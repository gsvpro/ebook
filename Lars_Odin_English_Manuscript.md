# Lars Odin

## Chapter 1: The Rim

Lars "Plates" Odin ordered a large bottle of Xaltor X whisky, four small glasses, and a Galactic IPA, and used the comm unit to pretend to pay at the bar; in fact he paid with his hand at the same time as he tried to hide the shock when all the information streamed in from the payment network, because he saw every weakness, bug, and attack vector. He could easily have emptied the bar of every galactic krona in the system, but that was sinking a little too low. He managed a small grimace. The bartender stared suspiciously at the whisky bottle while Lars pointed backward with his thumb at four worn-looking Abantu sitting at the far end of the dim, worn-out pub, lit only by flickering neon ads, while ungrounded cables hissed and the deep bass drum of a techno track moved the air. The bartender nodded, understanding.

An ordinary unmodified human would have gone into a coma from a small sip of Xaltor X. Lars did not have that problem, he did not know why, but he had to hide it, which the Abantu from the Pleiades could help with, and he was grateful. His expensive high-tech military boots clicked against the floor as he walked toward their table. He had bought the most expensive equipment available so he would look like a spoiled idiot who had no idea what he was doing. On his shoulder he carried a bulky, lethally effective cannon. It could send proton and gamma radiation away in devastating balls of hot plasma. Aphex Defence had given it to him six months earlier. Otherwise he had his black, slightly dusty and oily captain's coat, the worn work overall under it, and as the finishing touch a ridiculous pair of sunglasses with yellow lenses and pink frames.

He set the bottle down with a thud, greeted his old friends politely, uncorked it and poured them a glass each, took a few deep swallows of IPA and then, unnoticed, poured a large dose of whisky into the beer. The Abantu had understandably become his best friends in the short time he had been on the station. Real Xaltor X was expensive and hard to find; that it existed here at all was a wonder. For a short while it dulled his brain, which always seemed to be doing double turns. It was as if he had several quantum computers built into his head; often he knew the answer to a problem before he had finished forming the question.

In some strange way, every life form in the galaxy that had reached self-awareness and technological development seemed to call itself human, even if it had no shoes, and the planet it came from Earth, and the planetary system the solar system, each in its own language. Sometimes that caused serious confusion. One could believe the Abantu had learned the name from Earth's Abantu, or that it was the universe playing a trick.

He pretended to use the comm unit to translate.

"Cheers, my friends. How was the shift?"

The Abantu nearest on the left, who had an unpronounceable name, cleared his throat.

"Heavy day. We had to make quota today or they dock the pay."

"Did you make it?"

"No. A plasma shovel failed and threw the whole mining robot off course. By the time we got that sorted we had no chance."

"Hm. I probably have a solution for that."

Lars made a grimace, wrote quickly on a napkin, and pushed it across, wondering at the same time whether the whisky was watered down.

The Abantu read the napkin and thought about it.

"That ought to work. Have you done mining? You don't look like a miner."

A strand of smoke that smelled of bakelite passed in front of Lars, the music seemed to drop, and he drifted back to the laboratory he had rented on the station around the super-Earth LP 890-9c. The SPECULOOS consortium had built the station, and it was ideal for research and manufacturing. For four months he had been building his own freight ship. He had quickly tired of the influencer role, invested some of his billions in quantum crypto, and left the rest of the Galactic kronor to earn interest in the bank.

He had felt it was urgent to build the ship and get away, he did not know why. Something gnawed at him. Partly it was the different voices, which were more like thoughts he could not get rid of, and then the small things: objects that had moved, people who seemed to follow him. Over the last few years several high-ranking company directors had also met with strange accidents. There was a worrying pattern.

He had worked up to fourteen hours a day in the lab and then let the robots and 3D printers spit out parts and modules for the rest of the cycle. Sometimes he had to go down to the planet and visit the high-tech bazaars to buy illegal parts that could not be traced. The amount of rare metals needed for the hull and the antimatter assembly he had to mine out in the asteroid belt. He had developed methods for surveying asteroids so he could find the metals quickly, and then he had to develop plasma drills and plasma shovels that one person or one robot could run.

Sometimes he went next door to Professor H. Fayolle, who researched for Aphex Defence, and they had coffee. One day the professor showed him a problem that was very nearly insoluble. There were simply too many unknown variables. Already before Lars had looked at the plasma-cannon module he saw the solution. A foolish smile started to spread, which he tried to hide by confusing the professor.

"It's fairly simple if you think about it. You have some fundamentals: thermodynamics, entropy, volume energy. Volume can be a problem, but with the right tolerance you fix that. Put them in a graph with three axes. Fairly simple math, but now we make a small twist, we put it into a spacetime graph, and then we transpose out a point with the golden ratio. This works surprisingly well with chaotic systems."

He had already begun drawing furiously on the board.

"You probably have the midpoint here."

Lars got a staring look back.

"We extrapolate a volume that is more like a cloud of points. This sphere, or volume, represents the space that is optimal without everything collapsing."

He started writing down a long formula.

"Here you have the purely mathematical expression. Feed in the values until you find a break point."

"The golden ratio!" The professor thought for a moment and made a few calculations. "That looks as if it could work. Why didn't I think of that?"

"Remember it's a small volume and you have time against you. You'll probably need to stop and start again after a few picoseconds. You see the time axis here, if this," he pointed at the spacetime time axis, "spreads outside the sphere, it collapses, of course." A little know-it-all, he added, "You find the golden-ratio everywhere in nature. It's as if the universe is playing a trick on us."

The professor had begun to mutter, then roared, "Merde! You've solved the optimization problem as well, not only the collapse problem. I feel like a student again. I have to sit down."

He sank onto a chair, chalk-white, with an empty look.

"If you look closely a few more problems are solved, I suspect. I also think you'll get higher performance with two chambers and let them work in tandem."

When the professor had recovered a little he asked, "Where did you learn all this?"

"I read a lot."

When the professor got nothing more out of him, Lars finished his coffee. As a joke he said, "Don't forget to put me on the patent."

"Take care of yourself. See you later."

He walked out of the fine white-painted, gleaming high-tech laboratory. Aphex Defence had been so grateful they had fitted his ship with the latest weapons technology. He was grateful for the hardware, but he could see it could be improved further.

Back in the pub he fingered the Aphex Defence Ragnarrök 5000 and said, a little absently, "Yes, I've done a lot of mining in the asteroids, in an earlier life."

He slapped the table, stood up, and straightened his coat.

"See you a little later."

With determined but slightly unsteady steps he started toward the door at the far end of the dark, foggy room. Glass splinters, peanuts, and rubbish crunched under his boots. A man at the nearest table, well past sensible drinking, was about to spill his beer. Everything seemed to stop. Lars stretched out his hand calmly, caught the glass, and set it down without a drop going over.

"Take it easy. This Belgian beer is strong stuff."

He got an angry look back.

When he came out into the large, half-empty corridor, the comm unit buzzed. The screen showed an incoming quantum-encrypted bitstream from Gabriel Arsenault. He answered.

"Lars Odin."

"Oui, oui, good that I caught you. I am Gabriel Arsenault, chairman of Aphex Defence. You now have a board seat, decided at the last quarterly meeting."

Speak of the devil, Lars thought.

"Do I have to attend meetings?"

"You can join remotely if needed, there are not many meetings a year. We have also transferred the first fees to your account, retroactively." With a little hesitation he added, "We are now number one in the galaxy in weapons, in part thanks to your groundbreaking ideas. The professor speaks lyrically of your coffee breaks, a grandiose Swedish tradition we have begun to copy in all the offices. We have also adopted your thoughts about selling only the highest-performance weapons to allies. We look forward to the next meeting, and remember, we take care of our own."

Before he ended he threw out, enthusiastically, "On récolte ce qu'on sème."

The contract arrived with a buzz. Well then, Lars thought, while the static rang in his ear. He sent the bank sequence unnoticed and got the balance up in his inner eye. He could not help a small laugh. 7.3 billion new Galactic kronor had landed in the account. Then I have even less reason to work, he thought. I already have forty-seven billion in the account. They must be doing well for some reason. He thought about the coffee breaks with the professor. Perhaps I made a certain impression. Then I can add weapons mogul to the greeting. He was considering how that would sound when he noticed a pressure change in the corridor.

A wall of hot plasma and gamma radiation was coming toward him at supersonic speed. The warning gave him time to choose a way out. A locked maintenance door to the right he opened with a quick thought, took a couple of fast steps, and got the door open as the deadly wall arrived. The heat was terrible, and it stabbed like needles in the shoulder he had just pulled in when he slammed the door shut.

The lights blinked on. He checked whether everything was intact. The sunglasses lay as a melted heap on the floor, his hair was smoking, the handle of the Ragnarrök had partly melted. There were long soot marks along the walls and a little smoke coming under the door. He looked at his shoulder. There were tiny holes in the graphite-kevlar coat.

From old habit he thought: if it is not immediately life-threatening, you will manage. That was not natural, he thought, while anger boiled up and he readied the Ragnarrök. He tore the door open and crouched in the middle of the corridor. About thirty meters away five awkwardly dressed military types in black armor were approaching with raised weapons. A pity they don't know better, he thought, and with precision he fired five fireballs. The result was devastating. Five heaps of smoking black metal were all that remained.

He walked slowly toward the wreckage and at the same time remembered to open a channel to the ship's biological intelligence.

"Grim, something odd is going on. Status report?"

"Right now you should perhaps first deal with the microdrone cloud further on."

"Thanks. But I have some idea of the immediate surroundings."

He sent out an EMP pulse that quickly burned the electronics in the drones.

"And don't shoot at the ones coming behind the drones. That's the cavalry, here to save you."

In the distance he saw as many as twenty people in gleaming Aphex Defence armor coming at a run. The first person reached him.

"Major Elliot Fabron, Eleventh Aphex Defence Brigade. We are here to extract you. How are you?"

Lars pointed at his still-smoking hair in the dark, lit only by emergency lights and helmet lamps, and answered, "Pretty well." Water from the fire sprinklers in the ceiling lay over the corridor like drizzle.

Fabron caught his breath and continued.

"Since 17:20 station time we have engaged and cleared out one hundred and fifteen terrorists."

Lars looked questioning.

"Oh. That was when I went to the pub. Who were they?"

"We suspect the Black Hole B-7 group."

"Oh," Lars said again, and went a little quiet. "That's the most vicious fascist paramilitaries in the whole universe."

"Why did they want a fight with me?"

The major glanced at the Ragnarrök 5000, smiled crookedly, and answered, "We don't know what they were after, but come with us now. The rest we go through when we are safe."

Lars began, slowly, to get angry. He ran a quick check on all security cameras, networks, and various wavelengths. He saw a swarm of drones in a maintenance tunnel on level 197, heading toward a gigantic freight terminal, which then vanished. The internal brain communication crackled; Grim reported:

"I have just cleared out some vermin, including ten Black Hole on the way to our docking station. I can confirm with certainty that it was them. They should all be gone now."

"Thanks, good work," Lars answered on the inaudible channel.

He cleared his throat and put on a grim face.

"It is a gigantic station, but you missed seventy-five drones and ten mercenaries. I have cleared them and can confirm your suspicions with certainty."

He lifted the weapon and held it out with the handle toward the captain. The handle had set, with long strands dangling and deep impressions from Lars's hands.

"I think you have a little room for improvement here. It's a bit flimsy."

"Let me see your hands?"

Lars was already walking, clapping the others on the shoulders as he went. He lifted one hand and angled it back toward the major. He heard something he did not quite catch, but there was not much more to say.

"Grim, how does it look around the station?"

"Two Black Hole frigates are beating the hell out of an Aphex Defence destroyer."

"Oh. Can we help them?"

"Absolutely. We have more juice than all of them together."

"All right, I'm on my way. Have to buy sirloin and wine later."

He ran quickly to a small hatch, opened it, and jumped in. In a few seconds he had fallen thirty-five floors down to the docking level, where he braked the fall by spreading hands and boots. He had not wanted to show the major his hands. They pounded and itched for a moment while he ran toward the ship. The gangway lowered silently and he ran on up to the bridge. He threw himself into the chair, strapped in, and shouted that it was urgent. Grim sat beside him and had already begun the undocking sequence. Now he put the engines on full. He turned his metallic robot head toward Lars with what ought to have been a grin on his lips.

"At least it isn't boring working with you."

"Is that so? You've got a job? When did that happen? I'm plotting attack vectors and taking targets."

Lars quickly programmed four tactical nuclear warheads of two kilotons and a hundred drones.

"Taste this," he said, and pressed the button.

Now they were in range for the plasma cannons. Lars began to work the two ships with the cannons, while the warheads and drones went out on different vectors in the elliptic plane. First the plasma cannons hit, then the drones, then the warheads, which they had not even detected because the direction was completely unexpected. At the same time he opened a channel to the destroyer.

"Captain Lars Odin, freight ship Fastulv. It's about to get hot. Start backing off, fast."

The destroyer's captain answered with a grim look.

"We have holes in the hull and we're on half power."

Lars looked at the tactical screen and saw that the two frigates had begun evasive maneuvers away from the destroyer. He looked at Grim, who had already begun to increase speed and change to a course that would place them between the radiation radius and the destroyer.

"Will we hold?"

At the same time two white, blinding explosions showed.

"No problem. The blast is very limited in vacuum, but the radiation can cause trouble if you have an open hull and unprotected electronics."

Even so the whole hull creaked, and a burnt smell spread through the ship.

To starboard the Aphex Defence destroyer now lay drifting. A hole showed in the stern at one engine, and frozen oxygen and wreckage lay in a plume out from the jagged opening.

"Maybe a little excessive, with nuclear weapons?"

"I am thoroughly irritated at having to nanny other people, especially when they claim they are here to help us. So I did not want a slow and drawn-out process."

"Ha ha ha," said Grim, with a tinny laugh. "Exactly your style."

To port the two frigates continued to destroy themselves with internal explosions.

"But I have to say it was a little charming that they wanted to save us. Can we go back and buy sirloin now, before they close?"

Grim looked at him.

"Under galactic space law we are obliged to assist in a wreck."

"All right. So you don't think it was enough that we burned off two billion in ammunition and saved the bacon of one of the world's largest military industries?"

"When you put it that way, you have a certain point."

"Now we go," Lars said, and rubbed his face. "I have to buy new sunglasses too."

## Chapter 2: The Farmer

They went through the connecting corridor toward the marketplace, which was like a gigantic sphere. They stepped in. Clouds sailed around in the sky, birds were singing, and high up on the other side Lars glimpsed a grazing herd of cows. He pointed.

"That's where we're going."

Lars opened the gate to the farm, sighed with relief, and walked along the gravel path. He whispered to Grim, who walked beside him with whirring servo motors.

"Now you have to behave a bit stiff and tinny."

Grim stared back with a sour look.

"Why? Do I get to eat anyway?"

"Yes you do, but we don't want to show all our cards."

Grim had originally been a ship computer built with human brain cells, fed with a nutrient solution. It had started as a discussion one day two months earlier. Grim had said he wanted to be able to eat as well, and to taste things.

Lars had answered that he did not need that, it was only an evolutionary survival strategy so we would not poison ourselves, but if he absolutely wanted it he had a free hand. Draw up a sustainable plan and they would look at it later.

The next day Grim had a thoroughly worked-out solid plan with drawings and everything. They were in orbit around Alpha Boötis to take on fuel. The red glow lit up the cabin. Lars stood examining the drawings on the screen. The ghostly red light reflected in his face.

"Good. You've thought about checking for toxic substances," he said. "And there's a shopping list."

"Most of it, probably all of it, can be 3D printed," Grim answered over the comm.

Two days later, when Lars walked through the connecting passage from the engine room to the bridge, he smelled cooking and heard smacking from inside the computer room. On the table by the keyboard stood a plate of pepper steak and potato gratin, and a metallic mouth was busily shoveling in meat with small manipulator arms.

"Fantastic. I see you need proper robot arms and eyes. But you ought to practice etiquette."

"I was so eager to test it that I asked the cook to make it. What an experience."

"But no Xaltor X whisky or wine with it. Your brain can't take that, and we have to ration the food. You can have half of the sirloin, the salmon, and the rhubarb compote, but the rest will be astronaut food. All right, think about how we can build you into a robot."

Lars's stomach had started to rumble, but he finished first by checking how the helium collection was going.

Now he looked up and saw a farmer in a hat, red-checked shirt, denim overalls, and green boots coming toward them.

"Welcome. Vigor Hallman. What can I do for you?"

They introduced themselves, and Lars continued.

"We're looking for farm sales of beef."

The farmer nodded and peered at them with a sly look.

"Then you've come to the right place. Do you want to eat here too?"

"Gladly, if that's all right. And we were thinking of a large load to stock the ship, closer to sixty kilos."

"Come this way," said the farmer, turned, and started walking. They went around the main building and over to a sales room. Straw crunched under their boots when they stepped into the timber cottage. It smelled of straw from the straw-covered floor, and of vegetables. Dried meat hung from the ceiling. In cooled display shelves lay vacuum-packed meat in long rows, and there were plenty of vegetables and root crops for sale. Wine bottles stood lined up along the wall shelves. Robots served the customers quickly but with care.

"We have everything," Vigor said. "We are completely self-sufficient."

"Oh. You've got something big going on, I see."

Vigor looked at Lars, laughed, and said, "People here appreciate real well-cooked food without semi-manufactured stuff. Three years ago I was close to panic. Demand exceeded supply. I couldn't keep up and thought about shutting the whole thing down. But I couldn't do that, so I made a plan to automate and industrialize most of it, and now I can spend more time with the animals as well, and we have fivefolded the sales."

"Wow. What marbling," Lars said.

They walked around and pointed at what they wanted, then the farmer chased them out onto the lawn outside, where there were tables and chairs under parasols, and said they should sit down while the robots packed it up. He paused.

"You can be my guests. If you have a moment, I'll buy you dinner. We don't often have guests from far away."

He glanced at Grim and asked Lars, "Does the robot eat too?"

"Yes, unfortunately. He's picked up a few habits."

Grim said, "I am not an it, and we would like pepper-fried sirloin and potato gratin if that's possible."

Vigor clapped his hands.

"Fantastic. Very good choice. We'll arrange that. In the meantime I offer you a surprise."

Two waiter robots in white clothes came out through the door carrying trays with two whisky glasses and a cordial glass, and set a whisky glass in front of Lars and a cordial glass in front of Grim. The farmer sat down opposite where the robot had set the last glass. Another robot came out and set down a jug of elderflower cordial and an unmarked bottle of whisky, glanced at Grim, and then went in.

Lars stiffened when he recognized the shape of the bottle. Upset, he hissed at Vigor.

"Is this some kind of test? I have had enough ridiculous stunts today."

The farmer peered back slyly under the hat.

"It is not a test, and you can be calm. You are among friends. I recognize a like-minded person when I see one."

Lars had begun to tremble a little, so Vigor continued.

"I can tell you that the whole farm is protected against eavesdropping and espionage, and we can defend ourselves, maybe not as well as you." He went on, glancing at Lars's Ragnarrök 5000. "The robots have several roles. They help with patrolling and various guard duties. I am not only a simple farmer myself. I am also an adviser to the station's governor."

Quickly he showed that the beard was a false beard.

Lars began to get the anger under control. He had scanned the surroundings and had discovered a few well-hidden defensive positions. He began to see the small details: the robots' precision, Vigor's precise catlike movements, which he tried to hide, just as Lars himself had learned. Strangely, Vigor began to look familiar, and he got a déjà vu, as if he had sat here before in a vanished time. He must not think about that, or a disabling headache would arrive as surely as a letter. He already felt a diffuse pain in his neck.

He leaned back, took a deep breath, let it out, rubbed his face, and said, "Sorry for my abrupt manner. It has been a long day. Let's toast."

They enjoyed the dulling effect of the Xaltor X. Grim sipped the cordial and said after a while, "What is this juice called? I have never drunk anything better."

"Elderflower cordial," said the farmer, and laughed.

"Ohh, can we take a hundred liters of elderflower cordial, Lars?"

"Of course," said Lars and Vigor in unison.

Lars had taken off the graphite-kevlar composite coat and inspected the shoulder. The work overall had melted into the coat exactly where the plasma and radiation had hit him. At last he got the coat free and could inspect the shoulder. It looked as usual. Not a scratch.

"Stay there. I'll be right back," said the farmer.

Lars lay down on the lawn and looked up at the clouds and the swallows. Grim lay down beside him.

"What a green oasis, on the edge of the KBC Void."

Lars avoided saying, or thinking, that he had never felt more at home than now.

He woke with a start. For a microsecond he wondered where he was, until he smelled the pepper-fried sirloin. He opened his eyes and looked over toward the table, where a row of robots had begun to set places. He knocked on Grim, which produced a tinny sound, and whispered, "Have you fallen asleep?" Grim had a funny habit of always imitating what he did. At the same time he stretched so it creaked, and walked toward the farmer, who had just come out through the door.

Vigor said, "Now you will taste the KBC Void's, and probably the galaxy's, best sirloin."

Lars glanced irritably at Grim, who with precise catlike movements had already passed him and sat down at the table without even a squeaking servo. Lars pulled out his chair, sat down, and leaned toward Grim. With a serious face he said, "That sort of thing can have serious consequences, not only for you. Never drop your guard."

Grim looked at him but could not get a sound out. Vigor peered at them and said, "It is easy for us, but hard for a robot who is barely dry behind the ears."

Serving robots in white waiter costumes poured wine and served pepper steak, potato gratin, and salad. They toasted and dug into the food.

Lars said, with sarcasm, "The only thing missing is live candles."

He had a blissful look when he looked up.

"This is probably the best sirloin I have eaten, if not the best dinner in a very long time," he said, with a dreaming look.

Grim turned his head toward Lars with jerky servo-squeaking movements and said, "I can probably agree with that completely."

Vigor chuckled and said, "That gladdens me to hear. I understood it when it got so quiet. Cheers, my friends."

They toasted with ringing wine glasses. Even Grim had been given a half-full wine glass.

After dinner Vigor slurped his coffee and said, "So you are stocking the finest meat and wines for the next destination?"

"And elderflower cordial," Grim added.

Lars did not know why he was so frank.

"Yes, it will probably be a small trip. I don't really know why, but we feel a bit hunted."

Vigor lifted his hat a little and scratched himself.

"Well, you have sharp claws and good allies anyway, so it should go all right. If you need anything you can count on me, and on the station too."

"Got a request to billet the Eleventh Aphex Defence Brigade overnight. They were apparently stranded."

"Ah, Elliot Fabron. Well, he is not so cocky anymore."

They began to walk down toward the main gate, followed by two pack robots with carts full of goods.

Vigor said, "In a few weeks this sector will be crawling with galactic police and Aphex Defence forces."

"Then we'll be far away."

"If you see Elliot, can you point the way here?"

"Absolutely," Lars said, and shook Vigor's hand, and gave him a hug. Grim did exactly the same.

When they had come a bit out on the road from the farm they saw a small column of people in gleaming armor coming over the hill. A tight-faced Elliot met Lars's gaze. Lars pointed.

"See the gate over there. Follow the gravel path up toward the farm. You will be well looked after."

He clapped Elliot on the shoulder.

"Take care of yourself. See you a little later."

Lars, Grim, and the pack robots with levitating carts continued down the hill. The gravel crunched, swallows hummed in the air, and a cow lowed. Clouds sailed on the clear blue sky. A bit away, water poured from a waterfall.

They did not go back to the pub. They went to the ship, loaded the beef, the wine, the elderflower cordial, and new sunglasses Grim had already bought from a kiosk between a knife stall and a noodle stall, and filed a civilian departure. Eight minutes later they left that vector.

Lars did not say much. The back of his neck still ached, not sharply, more as if something behind a locked door had leaned against it. Ahead of them the KBC Void opened, an absence large enough that the instruments treated it as an error.

"Just a short look," he said.

Grim made a sound that might have been a laugh, or a servo.

"With you, that has never once been true."

## Chapter 3: The Dark Object

Grim kicked one of the antimatter reactor's cooling pipes. They were dripping with condensation, and steam rose from a leak you could not see. Lars shouted up from the cargo hold of Fastulv, the new freight ship they had just finished building, that it was still dripping in the ceiling.

"The weld is gone again," Grim shouted back.

Lars muttered something you could not hear and started up to the bridge. He had a fix for the leak by the second stair, and then the warning system beeped. He pushed off the bulkhead, sailed toward the captain's chair, did a flip and landed, and began issuing commands.

Grim left the reactor and looked over Lars's shoulder.

"Weird signature. I do not think I have seen anything like it."

"How many have you seen?"

"Increase the magnification," Lars said, "and take us into a half-parabolic orbit on a vector with delta 0.02."

They stared at the screens. Lars had calibrated Fastulv's sensors to his own eyes, and it was still hard to see what was there. The light bent as if something heavy sat in front of them, but the mass reading stayed empty. The ship's clocks disagreed with one another by a few femtoseconds. Heat looked like empty space. Radar came back with nothing. The laser range finder gave numbers that could not be true. The neutrino scan said there was a volume of something where the other instruments said there was nothing.

Lars swore and went to the nearest viewport, the one that only stopped the wavelengths that could damage the electronics. He drew breath and said, in a voice very unlike him, that it was a station, very old, and that it existed in several dimensions.

"Several dimensions? Now it sounds like you have got a hallucination." Grim had gone through six hundred years of old research about the Aerits, despite being barely a year old.

The word sat wrong in Lars's neck, a nail at the base of the skull, and he gripped the viewport rim.

"Deceleration with delta minus in five seconds. 4, 3, 2, 1, 0."

Grim had not strapped in. He hit the command console with his head, a light metallic clang.

"Was that on purpose?"

"Do you want us to crash into the station?"

Fastulv, still dripping somewhere aft, stopped short of the invisible surface by less than a hundred meters. Lars opened the harness and flipped backward out of the chair. Grim did the same. They collided at the exit.

"Is it wise to release the drones?"

"Depends what it is. My intuition says we should not."

Down in the cargo hold Lars put on the vacuum suit and the EVA module with the ion jet. Grim only needed oxygen for the brain, so he managed with a small emergency tube on his back and adjusted the right arm that had taken a hit at the exit. Lars checked the Ragnarrök and they stepped into Fastulv's airlock.

They left the new freight ship on small ionizing gas pulses and approached a surface that seemed to absorb all light. Something in that surface turned itself inside out at a steady interval, pale, then gone, then pale again. The helmet lamps did not help much. Distance became hard to judge. The suit range finders refused to agree with themselves. Lars did a little better if he followed the pale pulses, because the rhythm suggested where not to go.

The magnetic soles tried to get a grip and failed. They bounced and began going backward. They adjusted again with small pulses from the ion drive.

"Look for something that can remind of an airlock."

Grim pointed. "What is that over there?" A glow lit an area about fifteen meters away. They moved by pushing off where they got a grip on protruding parts of the hull.

When they arrived they met a bare surface surrounded by a bluish glow not detectable in the ordinary human visual spectrum. Lars's suit sensors showed almost nothing. His skin prickled under the gloves.

He pushed himself inside the glow. He had not been able to detect any radiation or wavelengths that pointed to activity. Once inside he perceived a quick scan of some microsecond with the same blue light, but the suit sensors did not react. It tingled a little in his feet and hands. Something in the blood answered. A square lit up with a frame of blue light at his right side, where he floated a bit above the surface.

He put his right hand in the square.

He stiffened when it felt as if something tried to search through his brain. He tried to resist, but it was useless. After a moment the square disappeared and a large opening began to form, at the same time as an inner voice spoke to him in an unknown language. He understood it was a welcome, and then, worse, that it was welcome home.

Grim followed quickly after.

They were both drawn into a gigantic airlock by a magnetic field or some other force. Behind them the opening slammed shut. Along metal-gray walls and ceiling, surfaces of glowing bands lit up in a warm glow. Artificial gravity now made one wall become the floor. They landed with a light thud. The suit confirmed pressure, temperature, oxygen, and no identifiable pathogens. The air was almost Earth-normal, but cleaner than any station air Lars had breathed.

Lars took off the helmet.

"The environment I recognize from four-hundred-year-old documents about the Aerits," Grim said.

The nail in the neck pressed again. Lars pretended he had not heard the word.

"Let us get out of this airlock. Over here there seems to be a command console."

They walked thirty meters to a console on the wall. Lars poked at the smooth surface until it reacted and showed a series of rows of symbols in pale green glow, some of them sitting a little above visibility, like weight behind the eyes.

"What do I do now?"

Before he had even started thinking he knew the answer and pressed the symbols in a quick sequence. A light headache began to arrive. A rectangular door opening formed on the wall to the right of the console and they stepped through. The door behind them disappeared as fast as it had manifested itself.

They were in an even larger chamber. You could not see the ceiling or the far walls, but glowing bands along the walls partly kept the darkness away.

"This is big. It must be some kind of hangar." With echoing steps Lars began walking inward toward the center. Halfway, after maybe two hundred meters, it stopped short with a thud.

Lars and Grim felt with their hands.

"It feels like there is a big spaceship here," Lars said, and looked closer. "It absorbs almost all wavelengths." He felt with his hands along the surface.

"Then it should be black," Grim said.

"I think the light bends around the hull so we see what is behind the ship. I can sense the contours."

"Is this when you just open and step in?"

Lars fumbled for something that could be a control panel. "This ship feels big. Then there can be a cargo ramp at the back or underneath, but there can also be a slightly smaller personnel lock on the side, probably closer to the bow than aft—"

He did not finish the sentence, because a pale blue rectangle appeared where he had thought it would be, near the bow.

Lars put his right palm inside the rectangle's edges. The same unpleasant experience seemed to repeat, but now he was a little more prepared, even if it did not help. Images sparked and died before he could hold them: a white corridor, a hand on a control surface, a name he could not hear without pain. He let out a small puff.

"Have they completely started ignoring the personal safety protection laws?"

Silently a door opening formed in the hull. Light came on inside, one band at a time.

Lars stepped through. He did not know this place. He knew exactly where to go.

## Chapter 4: The Sea's Last Song

This time a voice echoed on Galactic Swedish in the old ship's internal comm system.

"Welcome back, Captain Laurðinn. System diagnostics show that all primary and secondary functions operate within nominal parameters. No critical deviations have been registered during your absence. Storage capacity is fully intact, and all redundant systems are in standby. Time simulations have confirmed a continuous operation of 315 years, 7 months and 12 days. Lars had, unusually, trouble finding words for a short second.

"Thanks." Then he mumbled, "That is a damn big ship."

Grim turned his tin head.

Grim looked at him and said, "Havets Sista Sång, see you a little later." They rounded the old ship and continued into the gloom. Grim was barely a year old. Havets Sista Sång had been watching for three hundred and fifteen, and it had its own mind. It did not take orders from Grim.

He understood nothing, but the fingers seemed to live their own life and quickly pressed the right sequence. Silently and quickly a door opening formed to the left of the console. They stepped through. As before it was like an airlock, and an opening took form when the first had closed, but at the other end of the room. "Here we take left."

They continued now in a direction in toward the center of the station. A large spherical globe came floating toward them far away in the distance.

Lars took out and unlocked the Ragnarrök 5000. "What do we do now?"

Grim answered, "Probably a peaceful maintenance drone."

The sphere began to slow, a series of symbols blinked on the front, and it talked to them in Galactic Swedish.

The drone sphere turned around, or rather began traveling back from where it came. Lars and Grim looked at each other. Lars secured the weapon and slung it on his back and adjusted the new sunglasses. They began running after the drone, and Lars called, "What is your name and what is your task?"

The protocol name jabbed in the neck, but the drone was already veering through a wide portal to the right. Ödesvaka swung left into a large hall. The lighting was weak and blinked at regular intervals. It smelled of burned bakelite and ozone. Large machines stood lined up in the hall. They began walking further in. The sphere had now stopped. It said, with a serious tone, "I stop here."

Grim's left hand twitched, then twitched again.

"I am getting noise in the control," he said.

Grim stepped back into the corridor. Ödesvaka hovered beside him.

In a line out from the machine furthest left lay at least ten robots and five drones, twisted and broken. The machine looked largely intact except a large hatch that looked as if it had come loose. He had a human face, mostly. Tall, blue-pale, with skin that looked too cool and too strong. One side of the skull and neck showed integrated material, not a prosthesis in any ordinary sense. Long arms, broad shoulders, a face like a Viking statue. He spoke slowly, the way a man does when he has been doing this work for centuries.

Lars and Grim ran out to the workshop. After some searching they found what they needed. They calculated the problem.

"We need a counterweight." Lars called to Ödesvaka. "Is there any large work robot? What did you use to place the equipment here?"

"It was built in place, but we have work robots that can lift several tons. But we dare not sacrifice more robots."

"If the cables reach, the robot can stand at the door. Is there any durable place in the ceiling we can put the pulley in?"

"There is a beam inside the ceiling surface and a service space inside the walls. You can reach it through a hidden hatch in the workshop."

Lars lifted his hands. "Where? Ah, all right, now it opened. Bring the pulley and the tools."

The hatch had vanished when his left hand pressed a blank surface, a little before he had decided to press it. He took off through the service shaft and up into the ceiling gap. Heat increased as he crawled. The air tasted metallic. When he thought he was right over the machine he shouted to the sphere, "How do I open the ceiling?"

Lars pressed his hand against the surface. A small square of one square meter suddenly disappeared and he looked down on the machine below, wreckage, Grim at the safe threshold, Valdrun pinned under the distributor looking up.

First he fastened the pulley's one end with a cable around the beam and tightened the bolt, then he pulled a long line he had brought up through the pulley, tied it, and dropped it down through the hole in the ceiling.

Lars began to feel a bit dizzy from the fumes from the facility below, but he was done and hurried unsteadily back through the cramped space. He puffed; never had he felt this weak. With a hoarse voice he gave new commands.

"Grim, hold the end of the cable and give it to the work robot. I will pull the cable through now."

The heavy work robot arrived in the corridor like a walking loading crane. Lars took one end of the cable and stumbled forward through the pile of robots and sheet-metal parts and tied the line to the cable and began quickly pulling on the other end of the line; at last the cable was through the pulley and he began threading it around the fallen power-distributor construction. The surface was hot through the gloves. The suit alarm flashed yellow, then orange.

"It seems worse than I thought, you look affected too but not as fast," said Valdrun with a serious voice.

"I have never felt this weak. Keep your fingers crossed now." Lars coughed, turned around, and staggered quickly back while he focused on taking one step at a time. He recalculated while he walked. If the robot pulled now, the distributor would drag sideways across Valdrun's pinned side. He needed lift first, then a little sideways movement. He attached a second line, nearly fell, wrapped it around a floor anchor that surfaced when he kicked the right panel.

"Now, start pulling but carefully, we do not know what the pulley can take even if it looked as if it had the right dimensions. Ten centimeters lift. No more."

The work robot pulled. The distributor rose with a deep sound. Valdrun inhaled sharply.

"Hold. Guide line left. Slow."

The distributor shifted away from the torso by a centimeter, then three, then six. The overhead beam screamed. Lars stumbled forward, grabbed Valdrun under one arm, and pulled. Valdrun's free hand dug into the floor. His damaged side sparked once, then locked. They came free together.

"Release the load."

The work robot eased the distributor down. It struck the floor with a deep metallic boom.

For one second everything held. Then everything went black for Lars.

## Chapter 5: The Helmet

It beeps. Pip pip pip, what can that be? He hears voices, slowly he opens his eyes. An unfamiliar blurred face says incomprehensible things.

"We were really worried for a while, but you are starting to come back."

"How long?"

"Forty-eight hours, we had to put you to sleep," said Valdrun.

"Nanobots?" asked Lars, and grimaced when the pain began to spread from the neck up into the head.

"Ah, we will fix that, I have a solution you will like. Besides, you are a true hero who saved me," said Valdrun, and patted him on the shoulder.

Valdrun stood up. He was tall and powerful and looked like a mix between a robot and a Viking, with a somewhat unnatural blue-pale skin like a vampire. Ödesvaka hung half in shadow near the ceiling, symbols moving across the sphere, and said something about medical stability that Lars did not bother to answer.

Lars slurped the strong coffee from the cup and set it down thoughtfully. "Was it the fumes or the radiation that affected me?"

"It was pure chance that I survived. If the radiation had had another direction and the gases had not risen and been ventilated out, it would have been over for my part," said Valdrun, and drummed his fingers on the table. "It is starting to sink in now, we could have lost six hundred years of progress, actually more, thousands of years. The lineage could have been cut. We have been too clueless and not thought of all scenarios. We would have needed better redundancy. We are getting slack."

Directed laser would not have worked, too many planets and dust in the way. Radio would not have reached either, it spreads out and disappears after a couple of light-years, and besides it can be perceived by the enemy."

"How is that supposed to work," mumbled Lars.

After five minutes the machine shut off automatically. Valdrun clapped his hands.

A voice saying a name that was not Lars. He was small, running along a white corridor on this station. Stockholm sat wrong now.

"How old am I?"

"I need a much stronger coffee."

## Chapter 6: Stronger Coffee

The stronger coffee arrived in a cup that looked too fine for what it contained. Lars took a careful sip and drank some more before he said anything.

Valdrun sat opposite with his long hands around nothing in particular. Grim stood by the wall display, where the station had given him Lars's medical telemetry after Valdrun authorized it and Lars had complained for a while, mostly out of habit. The display showed cellular activity, neural bands, blood chemistry, and several layers Lars did not have names for and did not want names for.

"How much is still locked?" Grim asked.

Valdrun looked at the display. "Most of the life story. The first stage put back the pieces he needs to stand: where he was born, how old he is, the name that answers when someone calls him, that this station is familiar, and enough medical access so his own systems stop fighting the lock."

"Yes."

The outer surface you walked on was only the door. The real structure is folded inside a spacetime bubble. Lars looked at the map. It was too large.

"How many are here?"

Valdrun's face changed, not much, enough.

"Awake, now? Me, Ödesvaka, the station intelligences, and some limited biological cultures in preservation. You and Grim arrived from outside. Most of the housing is empty or shut down for conservation."

"Empty because everyone left?"

The word hid did not trigger the old migraine. Lars set down the coffee.

"Practical version."

Lars drank coffee. That was better than answering. The medical chair had given him one clean fact and a thousand dirty implications. Six hundred and forty-one. Born on Station Zero. Not from Stockholm. His name had been Laurðinn before it became Lars Odin. He did not feel six hundred and forty-one. He felt tired, hungry, irritated, and possibly under-caffeinated.

Lars's fingers curled around the cup.

"Alive?"

"Unknown."

Lars nodded once.

"You."

"Me."

"Vigor."

Valdrun's eyebrow lifted.

"Later."

"Fine."

"He likely did not know I was trapped. Station Zero's outgoing messages under watch-state are extremely restricted. Havets Sista Sång, the old ship, was still under Lars's old command envelope. Fastulv, the new freight ship you arrived in, could not have carried a message out either, not from inside the bubble."

Valdrun's mouth tightened. "Yes."

Lars stood too quickly, regretted it, and pretended not to. The medical display complained. He walked a small circle because sitting still had started to itch.

"Black Hole B-7 attacked me on The Rim. Vigor gave me whisky and beef. One word from Grim's old fragments nearly splits my skull. I leave for the Void. We find Station Zero. I unlock an invisible ship. We pull you out from under a two-ton distributor. I learn I am six hundred and forty-one, not Swedish in the way I thought, and that something in my blood got confused when the chamber tried to kill me."

## Chapter 7: Pluto Protocol 9

The archive lay below the medical ring and above the memory vault. They reached it through a lift that did not so much travel as change where they were; one moment the door sealed in the medical corridor, the next it opened into a chamber like a shallow amphitheater, black glass and white stone and space-gray ribs, with pale blue filaments hanging in the air like frozen rain. "Where is the archive?" Grim asked.

Valdrun pointed around them. "Here. The light is the files."

The filaments brightened when Lars stepped forward. Each one resolved into dense encoded structure: what the file was, who had opened it, how damaged it was, medical warnings, and a map of what sat inside. Most of it sat in light an ordinary human would not have seen; the visible blue was only leakage. Grim recalibrated his optics, for the fifth time that day, and muttered that he was getting tired of eyes.

Valdrun stood at the central table. "We start with Pluto Protocol 9."

Lars stopped. The word Pluto pulled at him, a cold world at the edge of the old Solar System, a buried object, a woman touching something she did not yet understand.

"How old is the record?"

Valdrun brought up the file stamp. "Original capture: 2032, Pluto artifact zone, ISEA isolated module four. Copied and annotated several times. This copy was sealed into Station Zero's staged reactivation archive after Alpha Fourteen began."

Lars did the math the way he always did, without deciding to. "Seven hundred and forty-two years."

The chamber dimmed and a holojournal formed above the table, at first a weak blue ghost, then, as his eyes adjusted, temperature noise, radiation scatter, camera correction, later overlays of microscopic emission, microtremor analysis, neural stress inferred from posture and voice.

Grim leaned a little toward Lars and whispered that he liked her. Lars told him to be quiet.

"We found the first machines in my bloodstream thirty-seven hours later. I am using the word machines because nanobots is both imprecise and emotionally unhelpful. They did not behave like infection. They did not replicate blindly. They mapped. Waited. Adjusted."

"Black Hole B-7."

"Among others."

"Earth Reclaimers?"

"Still present, in mutated forms."

"Syren Collective?"

Valdrun's face became grim. "Worse than present."

Lars rubbed his eyes.

## Chapter 8: The Barge

Lars woke to wind, which he had not asked for. He opened one eye and found himself under a hard yellow sun, lying on a narrow bunk inside a wooden cabin that smelled of tar, damp rope, old coffee, and engine oil. Something rattled above him. Water slapped against a hull. A gull screamed.

For three seconds he was sure Station Zero had drowned. Then the ceiling dissolved into a line of pale blue text: SIMULATION CHAMBER 3. REST INTERVAL COMPLETE. NON-BEEPING WAKE PROTOCOL SUCCESSFUL.

"Ödesvaka."

"Rest environment selected from recovered comfort index."

The chemical weakness from the chamber had retreated into the background, and the strange internal hum of his repaired systems had steadied. "Apparently."

"A good one?"

He remembered nothing specific, no childhood on Earth, no real barge trip, no single clean event, but the scene carried a composite comfort: yellow sunlight, engine vibration, cold water, coffee strong enough to make moral arguments. He said the word for the first time. It felt old in his mouth.

"Jevons," Lars said.

Megastations that needed synchronized supply chains. Gas giant cities that needed constant thermal management. "Of course it is."

## Chapter 9: Threshold Models

Lunch did not look like Valdrun's glass. It was herring, potatoes, crispbread, cheese, butter, and something Grim called historically suspicious, though he ate it anyway after installing a temporary brine-tolerance patch.

Afterward the barge simulation faded. The water lost depth, the sun softened, the deck became a grid, then white floor, then the clean oval of Simulation Chamber 3. The planning model remained.

Grim had turned Lyra's threshold archives into a layered working board. Each branch showed how sure the source was, how old the data was, and what it recommended. Lars had never liked certainty. Certainty was what people used when they ran out of instruments.

"Open the first file," he said.

Grim enlarged a band of amber indicators. "Hostile interest in carrier biology. Artifact black markets, unexplained disappearances of long-lived individuals, abnormal blood-analysis procurement, and pattern-matched attacks like the one on The Rim. Valdrun looked at Grim. Grim's voice flattened. "Two."

"It is considered prosperous."

"Nine Aerits."

Grim tilted his head. "That was in Lyra's later ethical constraints. Your phrasing was less formal."

"Good."

Ödesvaka hovered in from the chamber wall. "Consent constraint marked as captain directive."

"Call it a practical rule."

"Directive downgraded to practical rule."

Valdrun folded his hands behind his back. "There is one more immediate issue."

Grim expanded The Rim's traffic logs. "Aphex has secured the station sector. Galactic police have opened a formal terrorism inquiry. "To a competent investigator, extremely."

"Yes."

"Gabriel Arsenault owes me several billion favors."

"He paid you several billion kronor," Grim said.

"Different column."

Lars walked around the model. No migraine stopped him. No lock punished him. Missing knowledge felt like holes in a map. His thoughts avoided those places not because pain blocked them, but because the road simply ended.

"Send him proof of Valdrun recovery. Minimal packet. Guardian authentication, no station position, no detailed medical logs."

Ödesvaka's symbols flickered. "Approved."

## Chapter 10: Sideways Memory

Stage two began after food, hydration, two stabilization scans, and Valdrun telling him there would be no coffee in a neural restoration procedure. Grim agreed. Ödesvaka displayed a safety diagram that agreed. Havets Sista Sång, consulted as if it were an impartial authority, recommended no in language so polite Lars took it personally. He brought coffee anyway.

The helmet room had changed since the first unlock, or maybe he could see more of it now. What had looked like a medical chamber was a layered instrument: emitters aimed at the brain, tools that kept the machines in the blood in step, projectors that could show a memory index, and several systems that did not touch the brain directly. They measured what the machines in the blood did when memory moved. He could think that now without pain. He still disliked it.

"Relational memory," Valdrun said, lowering the halo over Lars's head. "Faces, trust chains, grief, obligations, old command reflexes. It will not restore everything. But it may reconnect enough that names stop being empty containers."

The pattern began. No migraine, no wall, no hammer at the base of his skull. This time the lock opened like a series of old pressure doors, each one equalizing before the next.

The first memory was Pluto, not Lyra's recording, his own memory of seeing the old Pluto logs centuries later, during training. He had still been young enough to think four hundred years sounded impossible. Lyra's early survival notes had been required study. Everyone had laughed at the line about arguing grand principles after nothing was trying to kill them.

Then the memory shifted. 2033, not through Lars's eyes, he had not been born yet. Inherited archive memory, shared during later education. Cosmic Pioneer and the modified vessels left Pluto through a hidden spacetime fold. They carried Lyra and the ISEA team away from Earth-side predators and into Heliostat. The portal looked like a region where geometry had stopped pretending to be Euclidean.

The memory snapped forward.

Astra Lumara. A moon under a Saturn-like planet, rings cutting the sky in pale arcs. Nexus Outpost half buried in basalt and ice, ugly at first, functional, always growing. Printers ran day and night. Biological labs smelled of sterilant and algae. Reactor rooms shook with prototype fusion assemblies. Robotics bays filled with machines that failed, learned, failed better, and were rebuilt by people whose minds ran too fast for sleep.

GRIN there was breakfast conversation: Genetics, Robotics, Information technology, Nanotechnology. Exhaustion. Someone falling asleep on a crate of polymer muscle while an AI argued with a cloned liver culture. Lyra standing in a half-finished lab with frost in her hair, saying they were making the disciplines stop pretending they were separate.

Dr. Erik Torvaldsson threw a tool across a workbench because a field equation refused to behave. "The drive is in superposition. It both works and does not work until Lyra looks at it."

Lyra, not looking up from a reactor diagnostic, said that was not what superposition meant. Pierre said thank you. Lyra pointed at him without looking up and told him he was not helping. Someone laughed. The memory warmed.

The Kalari arrived as light and pattern first. Not Xal'thror, that had been an early human misnaming from bad translation and fear. Kalari came later, after enough contact to learn the sound-shape they preferred. Old, patient, pacifist without being weak. They did not think in command chains. They thought in gardens, weather systems, slow mathematics. Their consensus models made human diplomacy look like children throwing tools. They liked Lyra. They distrusted Pierre on principle. This also made Lars like them.

The Kalari gave them better questions rather than answers. Why build one base? Why route all power through one control hierarchy? Why treat intelligence as a crown rather than a mycelium? Why assume a civilization is more advanced because it burns more?

The memory jumped again. Years passed in fragments. Nexus Outpost became a network, then a cluster, then a pattern exported to small moons, asteroid habitats, ocean caves, mobile workshops, and quiet stations that did not look important from the outside. Fusion systems matured. Energy loops tightened. Early FTL vessels moved from terrifying prototypes to merely dangerous engineering projects. AI commanders became necessary. Relativistic combat and FTL projectiles did not leave room for heroic captains with slow hands.

The first NUKEA precursor appeared in a memory from the 2060s, not the later corporate consortium, not yet. A licensing shell and reactor standard built to distribute portable fusion and nuclear expertise, so no single faction could control the whole stack. Later markets would make it enormous. Later bureaucrats would put logos on everything. But the seed had been an Aerit answer to a simple problem: power systems had to be rugged, modular, repairable, and dull enough that colonies survived when geniuses were unavailable.

Lyra approved the standard only after making the engineers remove three elegant features.

"They make the system clever," one engineer protested.

"They make it dependent," Lyra said.

"But the efficiency gain—"

"Is bait."

That phrase stayed. Efficiency is bait. Not always, not everywhere, but often enough to be carved into the private engineering talk of people who had watched cheap energy become expensive fragility.

Another jump. 2070s. A room on Astra Lumara with Kalari light-structures hanging above a table and human equations fighting for space beneath them. Lyra, older now but not aged, stood beside four other versions of herself, not copies in the cheap sense, divergent continuities. Four Lyras grown from necessity, memory backup, risk distribution, and one ugly fact: one brilliant mind was still a single point of failure. They argued like sisters, colleagues, and one person disagreeing with herself across four timelines.

Lars did not understand how he remembered the scene until the restoration layer tagged it: archived guardian training memory, recorded from Valdrun's perspective. Valdrun had been there. So had Vigor, younger, without the beard, eating something from a bowl while pretending not to understand the equations.

Lyra-One said, "The portal network is not the deepest layer."

Lyra-Three said, "We keep treating travel as movement through space. What if the artifact is teaching access, not transit?"

Lyra-Two shook her head. "That sounds like philosophy."

Lyra-Four said, "No. It sounds like signal theory."

Above them, Kalari light folded into a pattern like ripples crossing water.

Dr. Harrow said, "Consciousness projection is a dangerous phrase."

"Then call it something boring," Lyra-One said.

Pierre, older and still offended by most things, said, "Cognitive field displacement."

"Terrible," said Vigor.

"Accurate," Pierre said.

Lyra smiled. "Temporary embodied projection."

Valdrun's recorded voice said, "Too many syllables. In a fire, everyone dies before finishing the phrase."

"Consciousness projection, then," Lyra said. "We can apologize to philosophy later."

The room laughed, then it became serious. Model 3 formed over months, then years. It was not escape. It was engineering around an ugly question. If the machines in the blood could send a mind across a distance, could a person take control of a temporary body built somewhere else, and still keep the original body alive?

Model 4 had been rejected early. That version copied the person into new matter and let the original die. Paperwork, then an argument about whether the copy was still you. The Creators may have misunderstood that, or maybe humans misunderstood what the Creators had done. Either way, Lyra hated it.

Model 3 kept the original body. They put it in stasis, kept an anchor, sent the mind-state across, and built a temporary body from local matter or from nanobot stock they had seeded ahead of time. They kept a return path. No talk about souls. They measured whether the subject woke with matching memories and a brain that still held together.

The first test body formed on a carbon-rich moonlet beyond Heliostat's outer belt. It was short, black-skinned like polished graphite, hairless, and had six fingers on the left hand because a construction routine had interpreted "increase dexterity" with too much enthusiasm. Lyra-One opened her eyes in it, looked down, and said, "Absolutely not." Vigor laughed so hard he fell off a crate. Pierre said the sixth finger might be useful. Lyra held up the extra finger and said, for rude gestures, perhaps. The Kalari light-patterns shifted in what the humans had learned was amusement.

The test lasted four minutes. Lyra returned to her stasis body on Astra Lumara with full recall, elevated stress markers, and a formal complaint about local material constraints. Later tests produced blue skin and gill slits, a voice two octaves too low, and one almost-failure when the temporary body's sensory bandwidth exceeded the return buffer and Lyra spent nine hours smelling colors.

"We are tunneling through bad assumptions," Lyra said afterward, lying in recovery with three sensor nets across her skull.

Erik Torvaldsson replied that was also not what tunneling meant.

"Today it is."

The moonlet tests used seeded nanobot stock and local matter within reach of a relay cradle. That was enough to prove Model 3. It was not enough to travel. The harder problem came later. Lyra took apart the nanotechnology in her own bloodstream and learned to listen for sleeping Nomad networks in quiet places: ice, dead hulls, old Creator anchors, dirt with no host in it. When her mind found one, the hard part was not chiefly technical. It was a frightening passage into the consciousness field. Then, at the far end, she had to ask. The sleeping lattice had to agree to build a temporary body from whatever materials were there. Interface, consent, local chemistry, and whether the anchor would hold. She got better at that too. Not quickly. Not cleanly.

Another jump, smaller. At first it had been ordinary workshop talk. A sensor on Nexus Outpost refused to stabilize. It spat noise into three different diagnostic systems, each one insisting the other two were wrong. Lyra stood in front of it with a tool in one hand and a half-eaten protein bar in the other.

"Decoherence," she muttered. "Total decoherence."

Elias looked up from a coolant manifold. "The sensor or Pierre?"

"Both."

Pierre, from under the console, said something in French that made Jada laugh without understanding a word. Lyra fixed the sensor. The data snapped clean.

"Finally," she said. "Coherence."

Mei Lin repeated it later when Jada returned from a successful scout flight through a debris field.

"Coherence?" Mei Lin asked.

Jada smiled, raised a thumb, and said, "Coherence."

After that it spread the way useful workshop talk spreads among exhausted people. A greeting. A status check. A way to ask whether a machine, a plan, a body, or a team still held together.

Then came the first mission where nobody laughed. Marcus stood in a launch bay under red light, helmet tucked under one arm, while the team prepared to intercept a hostile probe before it could find the Heliostat portal fold.

"We go into unknown territory," he said. "Stay together. Hold coherence."

One by one, they answered. "Coherence."

Later still, Lyra used it while arguing with Pierre about the thing that did not yet have the name Återviens.

"It is about keeping systems in coherence," she said. "Not letting them decohere. Machine, body, memory, colony, team. All of it."

Pierre frowned. "So coherence is..."

Lyra looked at the board, at the failed simulations, at the survival loops that were beginning to become something larger.

"Everything," she said.

Another jump. The phrase Återviens did not arrive as a revelation. It arrived late, after years of arguments, failed colonies, repaired colonies, Kalari critique, Rakh'Na threat models, energy budgets, cloning ethics, memory loss, overbuilt systems, and too many simulations where the biggest structures died first. At first they used ugly terms. Resilience index. Civilizational recovery capacity. Distributed continuity. Adaptive survival loops. Pierre proposed a French acronym so bad three people independently deleted it from the board.

The word Återviens emerged in the inner circle, part Swedish, part old academic joke, part memory of return. Return-capacity. Re-becoming. The ability of a person, colony, technology, culture, or species to take damage, shed dead complexity, and come back without becoming a monster. It was not for public slogans, not then. In public, it became farming habits, modular reactor standards, old songs, children's stories, maintenance protocols, Abantu trade customs, station architecture, and a thousand other things nobody could torture out of a prisoner. Nobody knew they were carrying it.

In the inner circle, the seven used the word.

The memory ended on Lyra in a quiet room, sometime near 2083, the year the Rakh'Na came for Earth. She stood before a map of the Solar System, Heliostat, and too many threat vectors.

"Just now," she said, "all our options are in superposition. We can remain hidden, intervene, flee farther, or let Earth face what found it."

No one spoke.

"When we act, the wavefunction collapses. Then we own the result."

Valdrun's younger voice answered, "Then choose the branch where the most people keep breathing."

Lyra looked at him. "That is not a complete strategy."

"No," Valdrun said. "But it is a start."

The memory dissolved. Lars opened his eyes. The helmet hovered above him. His face was wet. Grim stood very still at the edge of the room. Valdrun watched him with the expression of a man waiting to find out whether a bridge had held.

Lars wiped his cheeks with both hands.

"You remembered?" Grim asked.

"Not everything."

"Enough?"

Lars looked at Valdrun. For the first time the face was not a stranger's. Not fully known, but no longer empty.

"Enough to know your taste in breakfast has been criminal for centuries."

Valdrun smiled. "Six and a half, at least."

For a moment neither of them moved. Then an old reflex rose through Lars before he could decide whether to allow it. He gave Valdrun the smallest nod.

"Coherence?"

Valdrun's smile changed. Not wider. Older.

"Coherence."

Grim looked from one to the other. "That was not standard Galactic Swedish usage."

"No," Lars said.

"Private greeting," Valdrun said. "Status check."

Ödesvaka rotated above them. "Stage two restoration successful. Emotional leakage within acceptable parameters."

Lars looked at the drone, then back at Valdrun, and left it there.

## Chapter 11: The Useless Junk Bazaar

The first thing Lars noticed was the gravity. Station Zero's artificial gravity had an almost invisible grace to it. This gravity held him down with tiny corrections in the knees, a polite oscillation in the inner ear, and a cheap harmonic under the floor panels.

SPECULOOS station. LP 890-9c. He knew it before the view confirmed it.

The super-Earth filled half the shuttle window, a storm-dark world with blue-white cloud bands and a terminator line sharp enough to cut thought. The SPECULOOS consortium had built its orbital station where research, manufacturing, and legally flexible procurement could all pretend not to know one another.

Lars sat in a small rented shuttle descending from the manufacturing ring toward the upper market spindle, and blinked. Had he fallen asleep in the helmet room? No. He could smell the shuttle: warm polymer, stale air, a coffee bulb clipped badly into the console, and the faint acid tang of engine coolant that the rental company had classified as within tolerance.

He looked down. No Ragnarrök on his shoulder, no replacement sunglasses, no black coat, older work boots, cleaner hands, a cheaper comm unit. Then, not now, and not fully either. The memory had edges too sharp for a dream and gaps too soft for ordinary recall. This was four months before Fastulv, the new freight ship, first left the station.

The shuttle comm crackled. "Your descent angle is ugly."

Grim's voice, but younger. At that time Grim did not have a throat, servos, optical lenses, or a face that could look offended. He was a biological quantum core suspended across nutrient tanks in Lars's rented lab, cultured neural tissue threaded through superconducting lattices, sensor relays, and a ship-control architecture Lars had insisted was not alive until it began criticizing his landings.

"Good morning to you too."

"It is station afternoon."

"You don't have afternoons. You are floating in tanks."

"Your glide path remains ugly."

Lars corrected two degrees before the shuttle's advisory system noticed, then deliberately added half a degree of sloppiness because the dock cameras were watching and he had spent months looking like a spoiled idiot who could barely operate his own expensive equipment. The docking clamps caught with a thump. Lars leaned back and let the recorded docking assistant praise him for a safe arrival.

"You ate four hours ago."

"That was station food."

Lars wanted pepper steak, not printed protein with pepper vapor, not vat-beef with marbling algorithms, actual steak, preferably from an animal that had known grass and at least one farmer who knew what he was doing.

"I asked for steak."

"We have high-grade protein flank."

"Does it come from a cow?"

"It comes from a licensed tissue line. Sir, this is a research station."

He sat with his back to the wall and pretended to scroll through investment feeds while the pub's networks spilled themselves open around him. Again, not intentionally. Payment systems. Kitchen inventory. Docking schedules. Private security rosters. SPECULOOS procurement flags. Professor Fayolle's lab power consumption two modules over, spiking in the familiar pattern of a man bullying a plasma containment problem.

He could see too much. He did not know why. He knew only that showing it was dangerous. The hypno lock did not feel like a lock then. It felt like taste, like preference, like a natural reluctance to look directly at certain things. Whenever his thoughts neared the shape of an answer, they slid away and found something practical to do. So he ate bad potatoes and let the forbidden knowledge work through his hands instead.

"Black Hole?"

"Insufficient data."

"Corporate?"

"Insufficient data."

Tell him I am busy, Lars said, and tell him to secure the recovered Black Hole equipment and stop asking questions he did not have clearance to survive. Arsenault's gaze sharpened. Clearance from whom? Physics.

Arsenault said he was asking Aphex Defence to help him conduct an undisclosed counteroperation inside their own structure. Yes. This was outrageous. Also cheaper than losing the person and discovering what Black Hole did with them.

Arsenault did not answer immediately. In the planning chamber the real model floated behind the false projection: Aphex nodes, audit pathways, legal triggers, internal security chains, Major Fabron's brigade, Professor Fayolle's lab network, and three possible locations where the priority Aerit might be buried under ordinary employment records. Gabriel. Another silence. Agreed.

"Deception moving."

Grim said the Aphex audit cover was established. Concealment had improved. The false trail to the wellness moon had already been queried by a Black Hole listening post. Grim brought up Vigor's response channel, then the blurred priority nine list. One entry sharpened a little more under the Aphex audit overlay. Name hidden. Location: Aphex Defence research dependency, Lagrange facility near a cold brown dwarf on the old French expansion line. Public identity: materials fatigue specialist. Age on record: forty-two. Lars stared at the estimate. Forty-two. Five hundred to seven hundred. Somewhere out there another person was walking around with a life, a job, habits, perhaps friends, perhaps a favorite bad pub. They thought they were mortal in the ordinary way. Maybe they had headaches they could not explain. Maybe they fixed impossible problems and called it luck. Maybe they were being watched.

"We go there," Lars said.

Valdrun shook his head. They prepare there. Difference? A great deal. They did not rush in as ancient saviors. They established cover, studied their life, identified immediate threats, and decided whether contact was safe. If they contacted, they asked. Consent constraint. Valdrun's rule. Lars nodded.

They would use Havets Sista Sång and Fastulv, Valdrun, Grim, Ödesvaka, and a man who had learned that his life was a cover.

"Coherence?"

"Coherence."

Grim answered too, less hesitant this time.

"Coherence."

Ödesvaka's surface symbols brightened. Mission designation: Hold Coherence. No one objected.

Outside the planning chamber Havets Sista Sång began warming old systems that had not moved in three centuries. In the secondary hangar Fastulv sat under the station lights, full of beef, wine, and illegal parts. The materials specialist in the Aphex records was still at work.

Far away, or perhaps not far at all, a temporary body stood in a locked room for less than two seconds and left behind particles that folded into nothing.
