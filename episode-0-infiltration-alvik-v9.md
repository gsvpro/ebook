## 10. Under the Bridge

They left the restaurant at 02:17.

The village was empty. The NUKEA truck had not returned. The dogs in the kennel were asleep. The streetlights hummed with a sickly orange glow, the kind that made everything look slightly wrong, slightly unreal.

Lars led them through back lanes and alleyways, staying off the main roads, using the terrain the way he had been trained — not by Aerit doctrine, not by FAFO manuals, but by instinct honed over centuries of survival. He moved like a ghost, silent as the suits allowed, his boots making no sound on the wet asphalt.

Grim limped beside him, the damaged leg dragging slightly but functional. The android had compensated well — rerouting power, adjusting gait, masking the limp with a shuffle that looked like exhaustion rather than mechanical failure. No one looking at them would guess the truth.

Sigrid walked between them, quiet, alert, her borrowed suit still dripping from the tunnel. She had asked no questions since they left the boathouse. She had not argued about the route. She had simply followed, calculating silently, noticing things Lars missed — a parked van with an antenna, a window where light had moved, a dog that barked too late.

They made their way down to the shore of Mälaren. The water was black and still. The rain slicked the concrete. Far to the south, the motorway hummed.

At 02:47, they reached the Tranebergsbron approach. The bridge rose above them like a cathedral of reinforced concrete, its arches lost in the rain and the dark. The air smelled of lake water and rust.

The service tunnel entrance was where Lars had remembered it: a steel grating set into the embankment, half-hidden by weeds and a rusted chain-link fence. The grate was locked, but the lock was old and the hinges were corroded. Lars twisted it off with one hand, the metal groaning and then giving way.

He pulled the grate aside and looked into the darkness below.

“The tunnel runs under the bridge deck,” he said quietly. “It is flooded. It opens into the Kungsholmen side. The water is still. We swim through.”

“How long?” Sigrid asked.

“About four hundred meters. Maybe five.”

She nodded. “I can swim.”

“Thermal layer under the suit,” Lars said. “It will hold. The nanobots supply oxygen directly to the blood. We can stay under as long as we need.”

Grim tilted his head. “My chassis is not hydrodynamic. The damaged leg will create drag. I will be slower. And I will generate a thermal plume in the water. Anyone with a sensitive detector might see it.”

“Then we go single file,” Lars said. “Sigrid first. She is the quietest. I will follow. Grim, you come last. If we encounter anything — a drone, a patrol, a sensor — we stop. We hold still. The camo will do the rest.”

The suits shifted mode as they entered the water: light-bending camouflage activated, thermal signature suppressed, electronics sealed. In the water they were effectively invisible — shadows in the current, seen by nothing.

Sigrid pushed off and entered the tunnel first, disappearing into the black.

Lars followed.

The water closed around him. It was cold, but the suit held the cold at bay. He felt the weight of the water, the resistance of it, but the nanobots in his blood adjusted his metabolism to the pressure. His vision shifted — the suit overlay painted the tunnel in sonar and lidar, stitching the darkness into a clear, blue-tinged map.

Behind him, Grim’s signature was larger — chassis heat, mechanical whine, the drag of a damaged leg. But it was there.

They swam.

Fifty meters. A hundred. Two hundred.

The tunnel was exactly as Lars had expected: dark, flooded, still. No ambient light. No marine life. Just concrete and water and the faint vibration of the city above them.

At 03:43, the tunnel widened. The ceiling rose. Sigrid broke the surface and pulled herself onto the concrete bank, water sheeting off her suit.

Lars surfaced a moment later. Grim came up last, requiring a slight assist to haul his damaged chassis up.

They were on the Kungsholmen side, in a small service alcove under the bridge deck. The walls were slick with condensation. The air was cold and smelled of lake water and diesel. Beyond the alcove, a metal staircase climbed up toward the street level.

“Check in,” Lars said quietly.

Grim ran a systems scan. “Leg is functional. Power at sixty-eight percent. I can continue.”

Sigrid shook the water from her hair. “No problems.”

Lars checked his own wounds. The thigh was throbbing, but the nanobots had closed the tissue within minutes and the inflammation was already fading. He could move.

He looked at his beacon reader. The signal from the Hornsbergstrand high-rise was faint, five kilometers north, but steady. Valdrun’s encryption was embedded in the pulse.

“The extraction point is five kilometers north,” he said. “We walk. No running. Just steady pace. Stay low.”

He led them up the staircase and out onto the street.

They emerged into a world of gray rain and orange streetlights. The road was empty. The buildings were silent. Far to the south they could hear the hum of the motorway, the distant wail of a siren, the sound of a world that had not noticed they were there yet.

Lars checked the route on his internal map. Five kilometers north along the waterfront, then east into the Hornsbergstrand district. The path was straightforward — no major intersections, no obvious ambush points. But simplicity was suspicious. In his experience, simple routes were the ones most likely to have something waiting.

He did not say that. He just moved.

They walked.

The rain plastered Sigrid’s hair to her skull. The thermal layer under her borrowed suit was working. Lars could see the color returning to her face, the stiffness leaving her joints. She was not cold. She was not tired. She was an Aerit, and her body repaired itself as she moved.

After twenty minutes, Grim spoke. “I have a signal. Not HRGrad. Something else. Low frequency. Military-grade encryption. It is following us.”

Lars stopped. He turned his head slowly, scanning the street behind them. Nothing. Just rain and wet asphalt and the distant hum of the motorway.

“Where?” he asked quietly.

“Two hundred meters back. A drone, maybe. Or a relay on a building. It is not transmitting. Just listening.”

“Can you jam it?”

“I can try. But if I transmit, they will know we are here.”

Lars made a decision. “Keep moving. No jamming. If they are listening, let them hear nothing. We reach the high-rise in forty minutes. Then we deal with the signal.”

He adjusted his pace to match Grim’s reduced speed, his hand on Sigrid’s elbow to steady her when she stumbled on a cracked section of pavement. She did not pull away.

The Hornsbergstrand district rose before them like a ridge of concrete teeth — high-rises clustered along the shoreline, their upper floors lost in the clouds. The tallest building stood at the western end of the row: forty stories of reinforced concrete, its facade pitted and stained, its roofline jagged with antennae and HVAC units.

At 06:12, they reached the building.

The lobby was battered but intact. The glass doors had been boarded up from the inside, the wood warped by water damage. A security console sat dead in the corner, its screen black. The elevator shaft was empty — the cars had been removed years ago, the cables cut.

Stairs.

Lars counted them. Forty flights. Each flight was a calculation: Grim’s damaged leg, Sigrid’s condition, the weight of the field kit, the time they had left.

“I can take the stairs,” Grim said. “But the pace will be slow.”

“Then we take the service ramp,” Lars said. “Service ramps were designed for heavy transport. They have better traction, and the lighting might still work.”

They found the service entrance behind a stack of pallets in a delivery bay. The door was unlocked. The ramp beyond was concrete, steep, lit by emergency lights that flickered yellow. It spiraled up through the building’s core, bypassing the residential floors entirely.

They climbed.

Grim’s chassis whined. Sigrid’s breathing was steady — a little labored from the exertion, but her Aerit physiology was already repairing the micro-stress in her muscles. She was not exhausted. She was warming up.

Lars’s thigh burned where the inflammation had settled, but the nanobots were pushing back, minute by minute.

At 06:23, they reached the thirty-eighth floor. The ramp ended at a steel door marked ROOF ACCESS — RESTRICTED.

Lars pushed the door open.

Wind.

The roof was exposed to the full force of the storm. Rain lashed at them from the side, driven by a gale that had sprung up over the lake. The sky was a uniform slate-gray. The city spread below them like a circuit board half-buried in mud — lights flickering, roads empty, the motorway a river of red taillights moving north.

The emergency beacon was tucked behind an HVAC unit near the eastern edge of the roof. It was small, weather-sealed, broadcasting on a frequency only Valdrun would know to monitor.

Lars checked the signal. Active. Uninterrupted.

“We have a window,” he said. “Grim, you and Sigrid get behind the unit. I will cover.”

“Cover from what?” Sigrid asked. Her voice was calm. Her eyes were scanning the roofline.

“From anything that comes up that stairwell.”

She nodded and moved to the shelter of the HVAC unit, her movements precise, her balance perfect despite the wind and the rain. She positioned herself to watch both the stairwell and the roof edge.

Lars stood at the door, watching the stairs.

At 06:25, he heard it — not a footstep, not a voice, but a change in the wind. Something had opened a lower door. The pressure wave reached him a second later.

He raised his weapon.

At 06:28, the first HRGrad trooper appeared on the roof access landing.

They had come up the stairs systematically — not rushing, but methodically, clearing each landing. Two troopers. They had expected resistance. They moved with the caution of soldiers who knew they were facing something beyond a standard extraction.

Lars fired.

The first round was non-lethal — a pulse charge designed to disable armor and electronics without penetrating the human body. The trooper crumpled against the wall, his rifle sparking, his comms dead.

The second trooper raised his weapon. Lars dropped him with a shot to the chest plate.

Silence.

Lars checked the bodies. HRGrad Milisen. But the armor was upgraded — Black Hole specification. A patch on one trooper’s shoulder did not belong to the militia: a stylized nebula. A corporate mark. NUKEA.

He stripped the troopers’ weapons — compact charged-particle pistols, military-grade — and pocketed them.

“Check in,” he said quietly.

Grim ran a systems scan. “Leg is holding. Power at sixty-eight percent. I can continue.”

Sigrid brushed rain from her face. “Fine. No problems.”

Lars checked his own wounds. The thigh was throbbing, but the nanobots had closed the tissue within minutes and the inflammation was already fading. He could move.

He looked at his beacon reader. The signal had changed. The passive beacon on the roof was now broadcasting an active handshake — tight, focused, from above. A shuttle. Incoming.

“We have a ride,” he said. “Hold tight.”

At 06:30 exactly, a shuttle dropped through the cloud layer.

It was small, angular, heat-diffusion lattice active, descending like a stone through silk. It did not land on the roof — it could not; the roof was too small, too exposed. Instead, it hovered ten meters above the eastern edge, a rappel line dropping from its open bay.

“Go,” Lars said.

He secured the line. Grim went first, descending with one good arm and one damaged leg, the line paying out smoothly. Sigrid followed, her grip sure, her movements economical. Lars came last, watching the stairwell, watching the roof edge, watching the sky.

The shuttle’s bay was small but clean. The ramp retracted as soon as they were inside, and the hull sealed with a sound like a held breath finally released.

The pilot was a silhouette behind a blast shield. The shuttle lifted without waiting for clearance, its thrusters whispering against the storm.

## 11. Board *Havets Sista Sång*

The climb to orbit was short. The shuttle punched through the weather layer in ninety seconds, shedding rain and wind and the weight of the atmosphere. Then came the silence. Then came the black.

Lars watched the receding planet shrink on the screen — Sweden, Mälaren, the motorways and the high-rises and the runestone drop point — until it was just another blue marble in the dark.

The pilot’s voice was synthetic, neutral. “Docking vector aligned. Contact *Havets Sista Sång* in thirty seconds.”

Lars saw it then: the ship, hanging in the black like an old sword left in stone. *Havets Sista Sång* was not small. Even at this distance, its angular hull was unmistakable — scarred by centuries of missions, upgraded a dozen times, still the vessel Lars had captained for decades. It was in low Earth orbit, close enough that the shuttle could reach it in minutes withouta jump.

The shuttle closed the gap. The docking clamps engaged with a soft thud. The airlock cycled.

They were inside *Havets Sista Sång*.

Valdrun was waiting at the inner airlock. Tall. Blue-pale. Broad. Wearing a flight suit that had seen better centuries. He looked at Lars. He looked at Grim’s damaged leg. He looked at Sigrid, who was already checking her equipment, her breathing steady, her face calm.

“Took you long enough,” he said.

Lars grinned. “We brought something.”

Valdrun looked at the trooper weapons Lars had slung over his shoulder. “I see that.”

He turned and walked toward the flight deck. “Welcome home. Now let us get the hell out of here.”

The *Havets Sista Sång* engaged its drive.

## 12. The Long Way Home

The ship did not stay in low orbit.

Within five minutes of undocking, they were past the orbital debris zone, into the black. Lars watched the receding planet shrink on the screen — Sweden, Mälaren, the motorways and the high-rises and the runestone drop point — until it was just another blue marble in the dark.

Valdrun plotted the jump.

“Coordination cell wants us at the next relay within twelve hours,” he said. “We have a window. If we miss it, the next one is in three days.”

“What happens then?” Lars asked.

“Then we burn the corridor,” Valdrun said. “And we hope HRGrad Milisen does not follow.”

He engaged the slip-space drive.

The universe folded.

Color became geometry. Distance became memory. The ship was no longer moving through space; it was moving through the hidden channels between stars, the rifts that Lyra had opened and that Aeriterna had kept secret for centuries.

When the ship emerged, they were light-years from Earth, in the relay corridor.

Lars looked at the navigation display. The route stretched ahead — a chain of beacon points, each one a jump, each one a calculated risk. There were many jumps left. Many dangers.

But they were alive. They were together. They had the asset.

And the mission was not over.
