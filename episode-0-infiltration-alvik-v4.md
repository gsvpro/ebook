## 10. The Rush to Hornsbergstrand

Ninety minutes was a lifetime in a city this size, or it would have been under normal circumstances. These were not normal circumstances. Every street corner was a potential ambush. Every parked car could be a listening post. Every shadow could be an HRGrad patrol with Black Hole-grade optics.

Lars counted the minutes in his head. He did not need a beacon to tell him the time; the nanobots in his blood kept a perfect internal chronometer, synchronized to the ship’s atomic clock light-minutes away.

“Forty-five minutes remaining,” he said quietly.

Grim limped beside him, the damaged leg dragging slightly but functional. The android had compensated well — rerouting power, adjusting gait, masking the limp with a shuffle that looked like exhaustion rather than mechanical failure. No one looking at them would guess the truth.

Sigrid walked between them, quiet, alert, her borrowed suit still dripping from the tunnel. She had asked no questions since they left the boathouse. She had not argued about the route. She had simply followed, calculating silently, noticing things Lars missed — a parked van with an antenna, a window where light had moved, a dog that barked too late.

They took the waterfront route, staying close to the lake, using the industrial buildings as cover. The rain was heavier now, the wind stronger. Mälaren whipped at the concrete shore, sending spray high against the old warehouses. The air smelled of salt, diesel, and the particular metallic tang of a city holding its breath.

At 05:12, Grim’s head tilted. “I have a contact. Left flank. Two hundred meters. They are not broadcasting. But they are moving toward us.”

Lars slowed. “How many?”

“Two vehicles. Look like HRGrad tactical. They have thermal sensors. They sweep in a pattern. They will cross our path in approximately three minutes.”

“Can we cross before them?”

“The bridge ahead is the only crossing point for another kilometer. If we run, we might make it. But running in this rain, on this terrain—”

“We do not run,” Lars said. “We wait. We let them pass.”

He pulled them into the lee of a shipping container stacked against a warehouse wall. They pressed themselves into the gap between container and concrete, hidden from the road by the bulk of the metal.

The vehicles passed five minutes later.

They were not ordinary patrol cars. They were heavy, armored, their surfaces dull gray to match the city’s palette. The lead vehicle had a sensor pod mounted on the roof — not standard HRGrad issue. This was military-grade. This was someone else’s equipment.

“Black Hole,” Grim said quietly. “Mark Three mobile sensor suite. They can see through rain, through light concealment, through simple suit-weave. If we had been in the open, they would have seen us.”

Lars watched the vehicles disappear into the gray. The rain covered their engine noise quickly.

“Twenty-five minutes left,” he said. “We move.”

They broke cover and continued north.

The Hornsbergstrand district rose before them like a ridge of concrete teeth — high-rises clustered along the shoreline, their upper floors lost in the clouds. The tallest building stood at the western end of the row: forty stories of reinforced concrete, its facade pitted and stained, its roofline jagged with antennae and HVAC units. The boathouse extraction point was three kilometers this side of the district; the high-rise was five kilometers further north. Every step was a minute closer to extraction, and every minute brought the clock closer to zero.

“Five hundred meters to the high-rise approach,” Grim said. “I am reading a passive beacon on the roof. Signal is weak but consistent. Valdrun’s encryption.”

Lars checked his own beacon reader. Yes — the pulse was there, faint and intermittent, fighting through the rain and the city’s electromagnetic noise.

“Stay sharp,” he said. “The roof is exposed. If they have already set a perimeter—”

He did not finish the sentence. None of them needed him to.

They turned off the waterfront and angled up toward the street that climbed the ridge to the high-rise. The road was empty. The buildings on either side were dark — evacuated long ago, their occupants fled to the interior or to the refugee camps that dotted the northern corridor.

At 05:41, they reached the building.

The lobby was battered but intact. The glass doors had been boarded up from the inside, the wood warped by water damage. A security console sat dead in the corner, its screen black. The elevator shaft was empty — the cars had been removed years ago, the cables cut.

Stairs.

Lars counted them. Forty flights. Each flight was a calculation: Grim’s damaged leg, Sigrid’s condition, the weight of the field kit, the time they had left.

“I can take the stairs,” Grim said. “But the pace will be slow.”

“Then we take the service ramp,” Lars said. “Service ramps were designed for heavy transport. They have better traction, and the lighting might still work.”

They found the service entrance behind a stack of pallets in a delivery bay. The door was unlocked. The ramp beyond was concrete, steep, lit by emergency lights that flickered yellow. It spiraled up through the building’s core, bypassing the residential floors entirely.

They climbed.

Grim’s chassis whined. Sigrid’s breathing was steady — a little labored from the exertion, but her Aerit physiology was already repairing the micro-stress in her muscles. She was not exhausted. She was warming up.

Lars’s thigh burned where the inflammation had settled, but the nanobots were pushing back, minute by minute.

At 05:52, they reached the thirty-eighth floor. The ramp ended at a steel door marked ROOF ACCESS — RESTRICTED.

Lars pushed the door open.

Wind.

The roof was exposed to the full force of the storm. Rain lashed at them from the side, driven by a gale that had sprung up over the lake. The sky was a uniform slate-gray. The city spread below them like a circuit board half-buried in mud — lights flickering, roads empty, the motorway a river of red taillights moving north.

The emergency beacon was tucked behind an HVAC unit near the eastern edge of the roof. It was small, weather-sealed, broadcasting on a frequency only Valdrun would know to monitor.

Lars checked the signal. Active. Uninterrupted.

“We have four minutes before the window closes,” he said. “Grim, you and Sigrid get behind the unit. I will cover.”

“Cover from what?” Sigrid asked. Her voice was calm. Her eyes were scanning the roofline.

“From anything that comes up that stairwell.”

She nodded and moved to the shelter of the HVAC unit, her movements precise, her balance perfect despite the wind and the rain. She did not huddle. She positioned herself to watch both the stairwell and the roof edge.

Lars stood at the door, watching the stairs.

At 05:55, he heard it — not a footstep, not a voice, but a change in the wind. Something had opened a lower door. The pressure wave reached him a second later.

He raised his weapon.

At 05:58, figures appeared on the roof access landing.

They were not just one or two. They were four — HRGrad Milisen troopers in upgraded armor, moving with military precision. They had come up the stairs systematically, clearing each landing. They had not rushed. They had expected to find something.

Lars fired.

The first round was non-lethal — a pulse charge designed to disable armor and electronics without penetrating the human body. The trooper crumpled against the wall, his rifle sparking, his comms dead.

The second trooper raised his weapon. Lars dropped him with a shot to the chest plate.

The third and fourth took cover behind the stairwell bulkhead. They returned fire — charged-particle rounds that sparked off the wall and singed the suit-weave.

Lars pressed himself behind the doorframe. “Grim, can you jam their comms from here?”

“I can try,” Grim said. He limped to the edge of the shelter and extended a forearm emitter. A pulse of electromagnetic noise washed over the stairwell. The troopers’ weapons flickered and died.

Lars moved.

He crossed the open space in three steps, the suit making him nearly silent, and took the third trooper from behind. The fourth turned — too late. Lars disabled him with a strike to the helmet.

Silence.

Lars checked the bodies. HRGrad Milisen. Black Hole armor. The insignia was clear. But there was something else — a patch on one trooper’s shoulder that did not belong to the militia. A stylized nebula. A corporate mark. NUKEA.

He stripped the troopers’ weapons — compact charged-particle pistols, military-grade, not the usual HRGrad junk — and pocketed them. Better than nothing.

“Check in,” he said quietly.

Grim ran a systems scan. “Leg is holding. Power at sixty-eight percent. I can continue.”

Sigrid brushed rain from her face. “Fine. No problems.”

Lars checked his own wounds. The thigh was throbbing, but the nanobots had closed the tissue within minutes and the inflammation was already fading. He could move.

He looked at his beacon reader. The signal from *Havets Sista Sång* was coming in strong now — a steady pulse from orbit, Valdrun’s encryption key embedded in the wave.

“The ship is in position,” he said. “We have three minutes before the pickup window closes.”

He looked over the roof edge. Below them, the city sprawled in the dark. The high-rise was isolated — too far from the next building for anyone to jump across, too exposed for a helicopter approach. The only way up had been the stairs they had just cleared.

But there was another way down.

Lars pointed to a maintenance balcony three floors below. “There is a rappel point on the east face. I saw it on the way up.”

“You want to climb down?” Sigrid asked.

“I want to get to the ship. The roof is a kill zone. If HRGrad has more than these four — and they do — they will be here in minutes. We rappel. Now.”

Sigrid looked over the edge. The wind was strong. The rain was horizontal. The balcony was a narrow concrete shelf with a rusted railing.

“Lead,” she said.

Lars secured the line. Grim went first, his damaged leg swinging freely as he lowered himself with one good arm. Sigrid followed, her grip sure, her movements economical. Lars came last, watching the stairwell, watching the roof edge, watching the sky.

They reached the balcony and unhooked.

The pickup point was a reinforced landing pad kludged onto the side of the building at street level — a flat concrete slab big enough for a small lander, surrounded by a rusted fence and a few abandoned cars.

The *Havets Sista Sång* was already there.

It was not a shuttle. It was the ship — old, angular, its heat-diffusion lattice active, its hull scarred by centuries of missions but still unmistakably the vessel Lars had captained for decades. It sat on the pad like a sword left in stone, waiting.

The ramp was down.

They ran for it.

The wind howled. The rain stung. Behind them, a new sound — helicopter blades, distant but closing fast. HRGrad had air support after all.

Lars pulled Sigrid onto the ramp. Grim limped behind. The ramp retracted, the hull sealed, and the ship’s systems came alive around them — the hum of the drive, the smell of recycled air, the soft amber light of the interior.

They were inside.

Valdrun was waiting at the airlock. Tall. Blue-pale. Broad. Wearing a flight suit that had seen better centuries. He looked at Lars. He looked at Grim’s damaged leg. He looked at Sigrid, who was already checking her equipment, her breathing steady, her face calm.

“Took you long enough,” he said.

Lars grinned. “We brought something.”

Valdrun looked at the trooper weapons Lars had slung over his shoulder. “I see that.”

He turned and walked toward the flight deck. “Welcome home. Now let us get the hell out of here.”

The ship lifted.

## 11. Through the Rift

The *Havets Sista Sång* did not stay in atmosphere.

Within five minutes of liftoff, they were past the weather layer, past the orbital debris zone, into the black. Lars watched the receding planet shrink on the screen — Sweden, Mälaren, the motorways and the high-rises and the runestone drop point — until it was just another blue marble in the dark.

Valdrun plotted the jump.

“Coordination cell wants us at Station Noll within twelve hours,” he said. “We have a window. If we miss it, the next one is in three days.”

“What happens then?” Lars asked.

“Then we burn the corridor,” Valdrun said. “And we hope HRGrad Milisen does not follow.”

He engaged the slip-space drive.

The universe folded.

Color became geometry. Distance became memory. The ship was no longer moving through space; it was moving through the hidden channels between stars, the rifts that Lyra had opened and that Aeriterna had kept secret for centuries.

When the ship emerged, they were home.

Station Noll hung before them like a pearl in the void — enormous, spherical, its surface scarred by the millennia but still beautiful, still alive. The station was hidden in a spacetime bubble, co-located with the void but inaccessible to anyone without the nanobots to recognize the doorway. Only Aeriter could see the airlock. Only Aeriter could dock without permission.

The ship glided toward the sphere and connected with a soft thud.

The airlock cycled.

They emerged into the heart of the station.

The architecture was not human. It was older — curves that seemed to grow from the walls rather than be built into them, light that came from everywhere and nowhere, a low hum that vibrated in the nanobots in Lars’s blood. This place had been built before humanity. Before the Skaparna. Before even the oldest stories Lars’s people told.

The station’s intelligence greeted them — not with words, but with a presence. Lars felt it brush against his mind like a cool hand. It recognized him. It had been waiting.

“Welcome home, Lars,” the station said through the neural interface. “Your clone-print is recognized. You have been gone longer than expected.”

“I had complications,” Lars said.

“I am aware,” the station replied. “HRGrad Milisen has escalated contact with Black Hole B-7. The situation in the Stockholm corridor is deteriorating.”

Lars paused. “How do you know that?”

“Mesh intercepts,” the station said. “Grim’s local network activity has been logged. I have been watching.”

Grim’s eyes flickered. “I was doing my job.”

“I know,” the station said. “And you did it well. The data you acquired has already been distributed through the coordination network. HRGrad Milisen’s supply lines are being disrupted. Their Black Hole contacts are being flagged to the proper authorities.”

“‘Proper authorities’?” Lars asked. “Who is that?”

“Not here,” Valdrun said. He placed a hand on Lars’s shoulder. “Come. There are things to show you. Things you have forgotten. Things the hypnosis hid.”

## 12. What the Hypnosis Hid

The walk to the awakening chamber was shorter than Lars remembered. The station shifted around him — corridors that opened where he expected walls, doors that stood slightly ajar as if inviting him in, light that changed color as he passed.

He understood, dimly, that this was how the station communicated: not with words, but with topology. The building knew who he was. It adjusted itself for him.

Valdrun led. Grim limped behind. Sigrid followed, still quiet, still watchful.

“You have questions,” Valdrun said, not turning around.

“I have many,” Lars admitted.

“Ask.”

“Why me? Why was I chosen for this mission?”

Valdrun stopped. He turned. His face was the same as always — blue-pale, angular, calm — but his eyes were different. They held something Lars had never seen before: grief.

“Because you are not a sleeper,” Valdrun said. “You are one of the Awakened. We hid you in plain sight because you were the only one who could infiltrate without raising suspicion. But something went wrong.”

“The hypnosis,” Lars said.

“The hypnos project was designed to protect us,” Valdrun said. “It was supposed to be temporary. A shield. But it outlived its purpose. And when it began to fail — when the nanobots started to whisper to you before you were ready — you became dangerous.”

“To whom?”

“To everyone,” Valdrun said. “A partly awakened Aerit is unpredictable. Your memories leak. Your abilities surface. And wherever you go, the dark forces follow.”

Lars thought about the influence that had dogged him for years. The feeling of being watched. The migraines that came from thinking too hard. The strange coincidences — the corporate takeovers, the monitoring, the sudden interest in his ship-building projects.

“They have been watching me for centuries,” he said.

“Not centuries,” Valdrun corrected. “Years. Decades. Since the hypnosis began to fail.”

He motioned them forward. “The awakening chamber is just ahead. Are you ready?”

Lars looked back at Sigrid. She had been quiet through all of this. Now she met his eyes.

“I am,” she said. “I have been awake longer than you think.”

Lars turned toward the door.

It opened before he touched it.

Inside, the chamber was round, bathed in soft amber light. A chair stood at the center — not a machine, not a console, but a seat carved from something that looked like bone but felt like warm metal.

“Sit,” Valdrun said.

Lars sat.

The nanobots responded before Valdrun touched anything. They knew this place. They had been here before, in a life Lars could not remember. They surged through his blood, warm and eager, as the chamber’s systems engaged.

Light wrapped around him.

And the truth poured in.
