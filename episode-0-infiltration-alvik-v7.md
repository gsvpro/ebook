# Episode 0: Infiltration — Alvik Drop (Part 4)

**Continuation of the standalone episode, set between Chapter 30 and Chapter 40.**

## 10. The Rush to Hornsbergstrand

Ninety minutes was a lifetime in a city this size, or it would have been under normal circumstances. These were not normal circumstances. Every street corner was a potential ambush. Every parked car could be a listening post. Every shadow could be an HRGrad patrol with Black Hole-grade optics.

Lars counted the minutes in his head. He did not need a beacon to tell him the time; the nanobots in his blood kept a perfect internal chronometer, synchronized to the ship’s atomic clock light-minutes away.

“Forty-five minutes remaining,” he said quietly.

Grim limped beside him, the damaged leg dragging slightly but functional. The android had compensated well — rerouting power, adjusting gait, masking the limp with a shuffle that looked like exhaustion rather than mechanical failure. No one looking at them would guess the truth.

Sigrid walked between them, quiet, alert, her borrowed suit still dripping from the tunnel. She had asked no questions since they left the boathouse. She had not argued about the route. She had simply followed, calculating silently, noticing things Lars missed — a parked van with an antenna, a window where light had moved, a dog that barked too late.

They took the waterfront route, staying close to the lake, using the industrial buildings as cover. The rain was heavier now, the wind stronger. Mälaren whipped at the concrete shore, sending spray high against the old warehouses. The air smelled of salt, diesel, and the particular metallic tang of a city holding its breath.

At 05:12, Grim’s head tilted. “I have a contact. Left flank. Two hundred meters. They are not broadcasting. But they are moving toward us.”

Lars slowed. “How many?”

“Two vehicles. Look like HRGrad tactical. They have thermal sensors. They sweep in a pattern. They will cross our path in approximately three minutes.”

“Can we cross before them?”

“The bridge ahead is the only crossing point for another kilometer. If we run, we might make it. But running in this rain, on this terrain—”

“We do not run,” Lars said. “We wait. We let them pass.”

He pulled them into the lee of a shipping container stacked against a warehouse wall. They pressed themselves into the gap between container and concrete, hidden from the road by the bulk of the metal.

The vehicles passed five minutes later.

They were not ordinary patrol cars. They were heavy, armored, their surfaces dull gray to match the city’s palette. The lead vehicle had a sensor pod mounted on the roof — not standard HRGrad issue. This was military-grade. This was someone else’s equipment.

“Black Hole,” Grim said quietly. “Mark Three mobile sensor suite. They can see through rain, through light concealment, through simple suit-weave. But not through ours.”

“Explain,” Lars said.

“The suits are running light-bending mode,” Grim said. “Thermal diffusion plus optical refraction. Anyone looking directly at us sees what the suit projects: empty street, wet concrete, rain. It is not perfect, but it works against anything short of a direct scan with a calibrated beam.”

Lars nodded. He had forgotten. These were not standard suits. These were Aerit gear, designed for deep cover in hostile territory.

Vehicles disappeared into the gray. The rain covered their engine noise quickly.

“Twenty-five minutes left,” he said. “We move.”

They broke cover and continued north.

The Hornsbergstrand district rose before them like a ridge of concrete teeth — high-rises clustered along the shoreline, their upper floors lost in the clouds. The tallest building stood at the western end of the row: forty stories of reinforced concrete, its facade pitted and stained, its roofline jagged with antennae and HVAC units. The boathouse extraction point was three kilometers this side of the district; the high-rise was five kilometers further north. Every step was a minute closer to extraction, and every minute brought the clock closer to zero.

“Five hundred meters to the high-rise approach,” Grim said. “I am reading a passive beacon on the roof. Signal is weak but consistent. Valdrun’s encryption.”

Lars checked his own beacon reader. Yes — the pulse was there, faint and intermittent, fighting through the rain and the city’s electromagnetic noise.

“Stay sharp,” he said. “The roof is exposed. If they have already set a perimeter—”

He did not finish the sentence. None of them needed him to.

They turned off the waterfront and angled up toward the street that climbed the ridge to the high-rise. The road was empty. The buildings on either side were dark — evacuated long ago, their occupants fled to the interior or to the refugee camps that dotted the northern corridor.

At 05:41, they reached the building.

The lobby was battered but intact. The glass doors had been boarded up from the inside, the wood warped by water damage. A security console sat dead in the corner, its screen black. The elevator shaft was empty — the cars had been removed years ago, the cables cut.

Stairs.

Lars counted them. Forty flights. Each flight was a calculation: Grim’s damaged leg, Sigrid’s condition, the weight of the field kit, the time they had left.

“I can take the stairs,” Grim said. “But the pace will be slow.”

“Then we take the service ramp,” Lars said. “Service ramps were designed for heavy transport. They have better traction, and the lighting might still work.”

They found the service entrance behind a stack of pallets in a delivery bay. The door was unlocked. The ramp beyond was concrete, steep, lit by emergency lights that flickered yellow. It spiraled up through the building’s core, bypassing the residential floors entirely.

They climbed.

Grim’s chassis whined. Sigrid’s breathing was steady — a little labored from the exertion, but her Aerit physiology was already repairing the micro-stress in her muscles. She was not exhausted. She was warming up.

Lars’s thigh burned where the inflammation had settled, but the nanobots were pushing back, minute by minute.

At 05:52, they reached the thirty-eighth floor. The ramp ended at a steel door marked ROOF ACCESS — RESTRICTED.

Lars pushed the door open.

Wind.

The roof was exposed to the full force of the storm. Rain lashed at them from the side, driven by a gale that had sprung up over the lake. The sky was a uniform slate-gray. The city spread below them like a circuit board half-buried in mud — lights flickering, roads empty, the motorway a river of red taillights moving north.

The emergency beacon was tucked behind an HVAC unit near the eastern edge of the roof. It was small, weather-sealed, broadcasting on a frequency only Valdrun would know to monitor.

Lars checked the signal. Active. Uninterrupted.

“We have four minutes before the pickup window closes,” he said. “Grim, you and Sigrid get behind the unit. I will cover.”

“Cover from what?” Sigrid asked. Her voice was calm. Her eyes were scanning the roofline.

“From anything that comes up that stairwell.”

She nodded and moved to the shelter of the HVAC unit, her movements precise, her balance perfect despite the wind and the rain. She did not huddle. She positioned herself to watch both the stairwell and the roof edge.

Lars stood at the door, watching the stairs.

At 05:55, he heard it — not a footstep, not a voice, but a change in the wind. Something had opened a lower door. The pressure wave reached him a second later.

He raised his weapon.

At 05:58, figures appeared on the roof access landing.

They had come up the stairs systematically — not rushing, but methodically, clearing each landing. Two troopers, not four. They had expected resistance. They moved with the caution of soldiers who knew they were facing something beyond a standard extraction.

Lars fired.

The first round was non-lethal — a pulse charge designed to disable armor and electronics without penetrating the human body. The trooper crumpled against the wall, his rifle sparking, his comms dead.

The second trooper raised his weapon. Lars dropped him with a shot to the chest plate.

Silence.

Lars checked the bodies. HRGrad Milisen. But the armor was upgraded — Black Hole specification. A patch on one trooper’s shoulder did not belong to the militia: a stylized nebula. A corporate mark. NUKEA.

He stripped the troopers’ weapons — compact charged-particle pistols, military-grade, not the usual HRGrad junk — and pocketed them.

“Check in,” he said quietly.

Grim ran a systems scan. “Leg is holding. Power at sixty-eight percent. I can continue.”

Sigrid brushed rain from her face. “Fine. No problems.”

Lars checked his own wounds. The thigh was throbbing, but the nanobots had closed the tissue within minutes and the inflammation was already fading. He could move.

He looked at his beacon reader. The signal had changed. The passive beacon on the roof was now broadcasting an active handshake — not Valdrun’s long-range pulse, but a tight, focused signal from above. A shuttle. Incoming.

“We have a ride,” he said. “Hold tight.”

At 06:00 exactly, a shuttle dropped through the cloud layer.

It was small, angular, heat-diffusion lattice active, descending like a stone through silk. It did not land on the roof — it could not; the roof was too small, too exposed. Instead, it hovered ten meters above the eastern edge, a rappel line dropping from its open bay.

“Go,” Lars said.

He secured the line. Grim went first, descending with one good arm and one damaged leg, the line paying out smoothly. Sigrid followed, her grip sure, her movements economical. Lars came last, watching the stairwell, watching the roof edge, watching the sky.

The shuttle’s bay was small but clean. The ramp retracted as soon as they were inside, and the hull sealed with a sound like a held breath finally released.

The pilot was a silhouette behind a blast shield. The shuttle lifted without waiting for clearance, its thrusters whispering against the storm.

## 11. Under the Bridge

The shuttle did not land on the shore; it could not risk the noise or the heat signature. Instead, it hovered over Mälaren, inside the Tranebergsbron arch, and lowered a secondary craft: a small submersible swimmer delivery vehicle, barely large enough for two humans and one android.

They transferred.

The swimmer vehicle dropped into the black water. The hatch sealed. The propulsion system engaged silently.

“We need to get to the other side,” Lars said. “The service tunnel is flooded. We swim through.”

“The tunnel is narrow,” Sigrid said. “And the water is deep.”

“The suits are calibrated for underwater movement,” Lars said. “Light-bending camo works better in water than in air. We are effectively invisible.”

The swimmer vehicle moved forward, hugging the concrete floor of the bridge arch. The water was black and cold, but the nanobots in their blood regulated body temperature perfectly. Their suits maintained a micro-barrier that repelled water, kept electronics dry, and bent light around their bodies. To any sensor above them — thermal, optical, electromagnetic — they were simply shadows in the current.

Sigrid drifted beside him. Her movements were fluid, effortless. She was not swimming; she was gliding. An Aerit body did not tire the way a human body did. The nanobots supplied oxygen directly to the bloodstream. She could hold her breath for hours. She could swim until her muscles failed, and the nanobots would repair them while she rested.

Grim was the problem.

“I can manage,” the android said quietly. “The damaged leg is not hydrodynamic. I have rerouted thruster output to compensate, but I will be slower. I will also be noisy.”

“How noisy?”

“My chassis generates heat. Underwater, that creates a thermal plume. Anyone with a sensitive enough detector will see it.”

Lars looked at the tunnel ahead. It was a black cylinder, barely wide enough for the swimmer vehicle. The water was still, just as he had expected. No current. No turbulence. Just darkness.

“We go single file,” he said. “Sigrid first. She is the quietest. I will follow. Grim, you come last. If we encounter anything — a drone, a patrol, a sensor — we stop. We hold still. The camo will do the rest.”

Sigrid pushed off the swimmer vehicle and entered the tunnel first. She disappeared into the black.

Lars followed.

The water closed around him. It was cold, but the suit held the cold at bay. He felt the weight of the water, the resistance of it, but the nanobots in his blood adjusted his metabolism to the pressure. His vision shifted — the suit overlay painted the tunnel in sonar and lidar, stitching the darkness into a clear, blue-tinged map. He saw Sigrid ahead, a faint heat signature, moving with a rhythm that was not human.

Behind him, Grim’s signature was larger — chassis heat, mechanical whine, the drag of a damaged leg. But it was there.

They swam.

Fifty meters. A hundred. Two hundred.

The tunnel was exactly as Lars had expected: dark, flooded, still. No ambient light. No marine life. Just concrete and water and the faint vibration of the city above them.

At 03:43, the swimmer vehicle’s nav-system pinged a change in geometry ahead: the tunnel widening, the ceiling rising, a faint current moving toward an exit.

Sigrid reached it first. She broke the surface and pulled herself onto the concrete bank, water sheeting off her suit.

Lars surfaced a moment later, then Grim, who required a slight assist to haul his damaged chassis up.

They were on the Kungsholmen side, in a small service alcove under the bridge deck. The walls were slick with condensation. The air was cold and smelled of lake water and diesel. Beyond the alcove, a metal staircase climbed up toward the street level.

“Check in,” Lars said quietly.

Grim ran a systems scan. “Leg is functional. Power at sixty-eight percent. I can continue.”

Sigrid shook the water from her hair. “No problems.”

Lars checked his own wounds. The thigh was throbbing, but the nanobots had closed the tissue within minutes and the inflammation was already fading. He could move.

He looked at his beacon reader. The signal from the Kungsholmen boathouse was faint, three kilometers north, but steady. Valdrun’s encryption was embedded in the pulse.

“The extraction point is three kilometers north,” he said. “We walk. No running. Just steady pace. Stay low.”

He led them up the staircase and out onto the street.

They emerged into a world of gray rain and orange streetlights. The road was empty. The buildings were silent. Far to the south they could hear the hum of the motorway, the distant wail of a siren, the sound of a world that had not noticed they were there yet.

Lars checked the route on his internal map. Three kilometers north along the waterfront, then west into the boathouse district. The path was straightforward — no major intersections, no obvious ambush points. But simplicity was suspicious. In his experience, simple routes were the ones most likely to have something waiting.

He did not say that. He just moved.

They walked.

After twenty minutes, Grim spoke. “I have a signal. Not HRGrad. Something else. Low frequency. Military-grade encryption. It is following us.”

Lars stopped. He turned his head slowly, scanning the street behind them. Nothing. Just rain and wet asphalt and the distant hum of the motorway.

“Where?” he asked quietly.

“Two hundred meters back. A drone, maybe. Or a relay on a building. It is not transmitting. Just listening.”

“Can you jam it?”

“I can try. But if I transmit, they will know we are here.”

Lars made a decision. “Keep moving. No jamming. If they are listening, let them hear nothing. We reach the boathouse in forty minutes. Then we deal with the signal.”

He adjusted his pace to match Grim’s reduced speed, his hand on Sigrid’s elbow to steady her when she stumbled on a cracked section of pavement. She did not pull away.

The boathouse district appeared out of the rain like a row of old bones — concrete structures leaning over the water, their surfaces stained and pitted, their windows shattered, their docks rotting into Mälaren. The boathouse Valdrun had chosen was at the end of the row, the most isolated, the one with the metal roof and the heavy concrete doors. The emergency beacon Lars had placed there four months ago was still broadcasting, its signal steady, its encryption unchanged.

He checked the door. Locked. He picked it with a tool from the field kit, the tumblers clicking softly. The door swung inward on rusted hinges.

Inside, the boathouse smelled of old concrete and diesel and lake water. There were two skiffs tied to the docks, their motors covered with tarps. A small cabin at the back, barely large enough for four people. A heater, old and oil-fired, still had fuel in the tank.

Lars closed the door behind them and barred it. He checked the windows. None of them overlooked the street; they faced the water. From the outside, the boathouse was invisible.

“We stay here until Valdrun signals,” he said. “Grim, you repair. Sigrid, you rest. I will watch.”

Grim limped to the cabin and sat down on the floor, beginning to disassemble his damaged leg. Sigrid checked the field kit on the table: rations, filters, thermal layers, beacon reader.

Lars stood at the window, looking out at the rain.

He did not think about the program that had kept him in the dark for so long. He did not think about the nanobots or the voices or the decades he had lost. He did not think about the headache he had learned to suppress long ago.

He thought about the drone signal behind them. The listening device. The fact that HRGrad Milisen had known where to look.

He thought about the counter-suit detection in the hall. The Black Hole-grade tech.

He thought about the fact that the simplest mission — fetch a sleeper, bring her home — had become something else entirely. Something darker. Something with roots deeper than any militia, any planet, any war he had ever known.

Outside, the rain kept falling. Mälaren kept moving under the bridge. Somewhere in the village behind them, a clock was ticking.

Lars watched the water. He waited for the signal.

He did not have to wait long.

The beacon on the roof flickered once. Then again. Then a steady pulse — Valdrun’s signal, encrypted, faint but unmistakable.

Extraction was confirmed.

Lars turned away from the window. He looked at Grim, working in the dim light of the cabin. He looked at Sigrid, methodically checking the field kit, her face calm for the first time since the hall.

He thought about the message that had started this. The request for support. The drop at the runestone. The seven days they had planned, the four days Valdrun had given them.

He thought about the word that had no place in the mission clock: Too late.

But it was not too late yet. The signal was there. The boathouse was secure. Sigrid was alive.

Lars sat down on the floor beside the cabin door, his back against the wall, his weapon across his lap. He closed his eyes and listened to the rain.

The regroup was over.

The extraction had just begun.

## 12. The Signal

The message arrived at 04:22.

Grim heard it first — a faint packet on the encrypted band, encoded with Valdrun’s key. He decoded it in silence, his damaged leg propped on a crate, his internal processor spinning through the decryption layers.

Lars looked up when Grim’s eyes flickered — a brief blue pulse that meant something had changed.

“What is it?” Sigrid asked. She was still awake, still watching, still calculating.

Grim transmitted the decoded message to Lars’s neural stack. It appeared in his mind like a memory he had not lived:

**Extraction point under observation. HRGrad did not hack the beacon. They found it physically. A ground patrol swept the shore at 03:00 and spotted the signal reflection in a broken window. They have not traced it back to us yet, but they will. Secondary extraction is the only viable move. Proceed to high-rise roof at Hornsbergstrand. Beacon is active. Window closes at 06:00. After that, I burn the corridor and extract alone.**

Lars stared at the text. The nanobots in his blood went quiet.

“What does it mean?” Sigrid asked.

“It means the boathouse is burned,” he said quietly. “Not because they broke our encryption. Because they found the beacon the old-fashioned way. Someone was in the wrong place at the wrong time.”

He stood up. His wounds throbbed, but the nanobots had already reduced the pain to a dull ache. He could move.

He looked at Grim. “Can you move?”

“I can try.”

Sigrid was already on her feet. She did not ask questions. She did not argue. She just picked up the field kit and slung it over her shoulder.

Lars checked the door. The rain was heavier now, the street outside a river of black water and orange light. He opened the door and stepped out into the night.

The message from Valdrun had one more line, buried at the end of the packet, encrypted with a key only Lars should have had:

**They have not linked Sigrid to the beacon. Not yet. But HRGrad knows an Aerit is loose in the corridor. If they catch her, they will use her to trace every node we have. Do not let that happen.**

Lars closed the door behind him.

He did not look back at the boathouse. He did not look back at the water. He looked north, toward the city, toward the high-rise that stood like a needle in the dark.

They had ninety minutes.

And every minute counted.

## 13. Dock with *Havets Sida Sång*

The shuttle lifted from the street-level pad and climbed through the storm. Within ninety seconds it punched through the weather layer into the black.

Lars watched the receding planet shrink on the screen — Sweden, Mälaren, the motorways and the high-rises and the runestone drop point — until it was just another blue marble in the dark.

The pilot’s voice was synthetic, neutral. “Docking vector aligned. Contact *Havets Sida Sång* in thirty seconds.”

Lars saw it then: the ship, hanging in the black like an old sword left in stone. It was not small. Even at this distance, its angular hull was unmistakable — scarred by centuries of missions, upgraded a dozen times, still the vessel Lars had captained for decades.

The shuttle closed the gap. The docking clamps engaged with a soft thud. The airlock cycled.

They were inside *Havets Sida Sång*.

Valdrun was waiting at the inner airlock. Tall. Blue-pale. Broad. Wearing a flight suit that had seen better centuries. He looked at Lars. He looked at Grim’s damaged leg. He looked at Sigrid, who was already checking her equipment, her breathing steady, her face calm.

“Took you long enough,” he said.

Lars grinned. “We brought something.”

Valdrun looked at the trooper weapons Lars had slung over his shoulder. “I see that.”

He turned and walked toward the flight deck. “Welcome home. Now let us get the hell out of here.”

The ship engaged its drive.

## 14. Through the Rift

The ship did not stay in low orbit.

Within five minutes of undocking, they were past the orbital debris zone, into the black. Lars watched the receding planet shrink on the screen — Sweden, Mälaren, the motorways and the high-rises and the runestone drop point — until it was just another blue marble in the dark.

Valdrun plotted the jump.

“Coordination cell wants us at Station Noll within twelve hours,” he said. “We have a window. If we miss it, the next one is in three days.”

“What happens then?” Lars asked.

“Then we burn the corridor,” Valdrun said. “And we hope HRGrad Milisen does not follow.”

He engaged the slip-space drive.

The universe folded.

Color became geometry. Distance became memory. The ship was no longer moving through space; it was moving through the hidden channels between stars, the rifts that Lyra had opened and that Aeriterna had kept secret for centuries.

When the ship emerged, they were home.

Station Noll hung before them like a pearl in the void — enormous, spherical, its surface scarred by the millennia but still beautiful, still alive. The station was hidden in a spacetime bubble, co-located with the void but inaccessible to anyone without the nanobots to recognize the doorway. Only Aeriter could see the airlock. Only Aeriter could dock without permission.

The ship glided toward the sphere and connected with a soft thud.

The airlock cycled.

They emerged into the heart of the station.

The architecture was not human. It was older — curves that seemed to grow from the walls rather than be built into them, light that came from everywhere and nowhere, a low hum that vibrated in the nanobots in Lars’s blood. This place had been built before humanity. Before the Skaparna. Before even the oldest stories Lars’s people told.

The station’s intelligence greeted them — not with words, but with a presence. Lars felt it brush against his mind like a cool hand. It recognized him. It had been waiting.

“Welcome home, Lars,” the station said through the neural interface. “Your clone-print is recognized. You have been gone longer than expected.”

“I had complications,” Lars said.

“I am aware,” the station replied. “HRGrad Milisen has escalated contact with Black Hole B-7. The situation in the Stockholm corridor is deteriorating.”

Lars paused. “How do you know that?”

“Mesh intercepts,” the station said. “Grim’s local network activity has been logged. I have been watching.”

Grim’s eyes flickered. “I was doing my job.”

“I know,” the station said. “And you did it well. The data you acquired has already been distributed through the coordination network. HRGrad Milisen’s supply lines are being disrupted. Their Black Hole contacts are being flagged to the proper authorities.”

“‘Proper authorities’?” Lars asked. “Who is that?”

“Not here,” Valdrun said. He placed a hand on Lars’s shoulder. “Come. There are things to show you. Things you have forgotten. Things the hypnosis hid.”

## 15. What the Hypnosis Hid

The walk to the awakening chamber was shorter than Lars remembered. The station shifted around him — corridors that opened where he expected walls, doors that stood slightly ajar as if inviting him in, light that changed color as he passed.

He understood, dimly, that this was how the station communicated: not with words, but with topology. The building knew who he was. It adjusted itself for him.

Valdrun led. Grim limped behind. Sigrid followed, still quiet, still watchful.

“You have questions,” Valdrun said, not turning around.

“I have many,” Lars admitted.

“Ask.”

“Why me? Why was I chosen for this mission?”

Valdrun stopped. He turned. His face was the same as always — blue-pale, angular, calm — but his eyes were different. They held something Lars had never seen before: grief.

“Because you are not a sleeper,” Valdrun said. “You are one of the Awakened. We hid you in plain sight because you were the only one who could infiltrate without raising suspicion. But something went wrong.”

“The hypnosis,” Lars said.

“The hypnos project was designed to protect us,” Valdrun said. “It was supposed to be temporary. A shield. But it outlived its purpose. And when it began to fail — when the nanobots started to whisper to you before you were ready — you became dangerous.”

“To whom?”

“To everyone,” Valdrun said. “A partly awakened Aerit is unpredictable. Your memories leak. Your abilities surface. And wherever you go, the dark forces follow.”

Lars thought about the ínfluence that had dogged him for years. The feeling of being watched. The migraines that came from thinking too hard. The strange coincidences — the corporate takeovers, the monitoring, the sudden interest in his ship-building projects.

“They have been watching me for years,” he said.

“Not centuries,” Valdrun corrected. “Years. Decades. Since the hypnosis began to fail.”

He motioned them forward. “The awakening chamber is just ahead. Are you ready?”

Lars looked back at Sigrid. She had been quiet through all of this. Now she met his eyes.

“I am,” she said. “I have been awake longer than you think.”

Lars turned toward the door.

It opened before he touched it.

Inside, the chamber was round, bathed in soft amber light. A chair stood at the center — not a machine, not a console, but a seat carved from something that looked like bone but felt like warm metal.

“Sit,” Valdrun said.

Lars sat.

The nanobots responded before Valdrun touched anything. They knew this place. They had been here before, in a life Lars could not remember. They surged through his blood, warm and eager, as the chamber’s systems engaged.

Light wrapped around him.

And the truth poured in.
