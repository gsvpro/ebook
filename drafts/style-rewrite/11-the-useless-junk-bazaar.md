## Chapter 11: The Useless Junk Bazaar

The first thing Lars noticed was the gravity. Station Zero's artificial gravity had an almost invisible grace to it. This gravity held him down with tiny corrections in the knees, a polite oscillation in the inner ear, and a cheap harmonic under the floor panels.

SPECULOOS station. LP 890-9c. He knew it before the view confirmed it.

The super-Earth filled half the shuttle window, a storm-dark world with blue-white cloud bands and a terminator line sharp enough to cut thought. The SPECULOOS consortium had built its orbital station where research, manufacturing, and legally flexible procurement could all pretend not to know one another.

Lars sat in a small rented shuttle descending from the manufacturing ring toward the upper market spindle, and blinked. Had he fallen asleep in the helmet room? No. He could smell the shuttle: warm polymer, stale air, a coffee bulb clipped badly into the console, and the faint acid tang of engine coolant that the rental company had classified as within tolerance.

He looked down. No Ragnarrök on his shoulder, no replacement sunglasses, no black coat, older work boots, cleaner hands, a cheaper comm unit. Then, not now, and not fully either. The memory had edges too sharp for a dream and gaps too soft for ordinary recall. This was four months before Fastulv, the new freight ship, first left the station.

The shuttle comm crackled. "Your descent angle is ugly."

Grim's voice, but younger. At that time Grim did not have a throat, servos, optical lenses, or a face that could look offended. He was a biological quantum core suspended across nutrient tanks in Lars's rented lab, cultured neural tissue threaded through superconducting lattices, sensor relays, and a ship-control architecture Lars had insisted was not alive until it began criticizing his landings.

"Good morning to you too."

"It is station afternoon."

"You don't have afternoons. You are floating in tanks."

"Your glide path remains ugly."

Lars corrected two degrees before the shuttle's advisory system noticed, then deliberately added half a degree of sloppiness because the dock cameras were watching and he had spent months looking like a spoiled idiot who could barely operate his own expensive equipment. The shuttle complained. Grim complained more carefully that he had overcorrected, and that he was being inconsistent about it.

The docking clamps caught with a thump. Lars leaned back and let the recorded docking assistant praise him for a safe arrival.

"I am going to eat," he told Grim.

"You ate four hours ago."

"That was station food."

The pub sat three levels below the legal bazaar and two levels above the illegal one, which was exactly the kind of compromise LP 890-9c specialized in. It was called The Tidal Lock. Lars wanted pepper steak, not printed protein with pepper vapor, not vat-beef with marbling algorithms, actual steak, preferably from an animal that had known grass and at least one farmer who knew what he was doing.

The bartender checked the menu system. "We have the mushroom cutlet."

"I asked for steak."

"We have high-grade protein flank."

"Does it come from a cow?"

"It comes from a licensed tissue line. Sir, this is a research station."

Grim spoke through the comm bead in Lars's ear and suggested the cutlet. Lars ordered coffee, fried potatoes, and something the menu insisted was a steak sandwich. He sat with his back to the wall and pretended to scroll through investment feeds while the pub's networks spilled themselves open around him. Again, not intentionally. Payment systems. Kitchen inventory. Docking schedules. Private security rosters. SPECULOOS procurement flags. Professor Fayolle's lab power consumption two modules over, spiking in the familiar pattern of a man bullying a plasma containment problem.

He could see too much. He did not know why. He knew only that showing it was dangerous. The hypno lock did not feel like a lock then. It felt like taste, like preference, like a natural reluctance to look directly at certain things. Whenever his thoughts neared the shape of an answer, they slid away and found something practical to do. So he ate bad potatoes and let the forbidden knowledge work through his hands instead.

"You are being watched," Grim said.

Lars did not look up. "By who?"

"Unknown. Tall figure near the rear service arch. Thermal profile partially masked. Face obscured. No station ID handshake."

"Black Hole?"

"Insufficient data."

"Corporate?"

"Insufficient data."

Lars took a bite of the alleged sandwich and regretted it. "Keep watching."

The figure did not approach. When Lars left the pub, it followed. It stayed outside obvious camera angles, used crowds, let reflections do work, and avoided the cheap mistakes of normal station surveillance.

The legal bazaar glittered under bright consortium lights: certified tools, licensed components, polished reactor parts from NUKEA distributors, drone chassis with warranties, clean booths selling clean technology to clean people. Lars walked through it without stopping.

The illegal bazaar began where the floor got older. No sign announced it. The station simply forgot to maintain a section of service corridor. Stalls formed from cargo crates, privacy sheets, old pressure doors, and projected menus that lied about everything except price. Here were military surplus servos with serials burned off, deprecated AI cores sold as art, Abantu mining tools modified beyond legal tolerance, black-market NUKEA-compatible regulator shells, three crates of cloned saffron bulbs, a Kalari pattern sculpture that was almost certainly fake and still made Lars's teeth itch.

And junk. So much junk. Old hull fragments. Dead interface plates. Melted sensor beads. Unknown alloy struts. Pale blue shards that looked like glass until Lars's eyes found the emission layer hidden below human-visible wavelengths.

He stopped at a stall run by a scavenger with too many rings and the relaxed posture of someone surrounded by hidden weapons.

"Looking for decoration?" the scavenger asked.

"Ship parts."

The scavenger laughed. "Then not here. This is museum trash. Weird old cult-tech. Pretty, useless, impossible to machine. Rich idiots like it on walls."

Lars picked up a palm-sized black plate. To ordinary instruments it looked dead, but it was only dormant. Its surface carried a molecular memory lattice so dense the public scanner beside the stall had classified it as stone. Lars did not know that term, not consciously. He only knew the plate had been part of a self-healing conduit controller, probably from a minor Aerit utility system, and that it could regulate Fastulv's antimatter cooling loop with less latency than any legal component on the station.

He nearly dropped it. How did he know that? His neck tightened. The thought slid away.

"This is ugly," he said.

"Collectors disagree."

The scavenger considered. "Five thousand Galactic."

Lars turned the plate over. "For dead wall jewelry? Two hundred."

"Insult."

Grim whispered, "That component is not dead."

Lars kept his face blank. "I know."

"How?"

"Later."

"You do not know how."

"Later."

He added three more pieces: a pale blue strip the scavenger called alien trim, two fist-sized nodules sold as paperweights, and a bundle of filament that looked like burnt spider silk. The scavenger thought he was unloading unsellable curiosities.

Then he saw the shard. It sat under a cracked transparent cover at the back of the stall, half buried under obsolete gravitic couplers, black at one angle, space-gray at another, with a faint blue line that ordinary eyes would miss. His hand moved toward it. The dark figure across the corridor shifted. Lars stopped.

"That one?" the scavenger said. "Pure junk. Can't cut it, can't melt it, can't scan it. Creeps out customers, which is why I keep it."

They settled on eight hundred Galactic for the shard and the rest of the useless junk. Lars paid through a visible comm transfer this time, no hand trick, no lattice intrusion, too many eyes. The dark figure was gone. That bothered him more.

On the shuttle back to the SPECULOOS lab ring, Grim ran passive analysis through the cargo crate sensors. "Most of what you bought should not exist in the manufacturing record."

"Illegal parts often have that quality."

"No. I mean the material behavior is inconsistent with contemporary science. The black plate is responding to your proximity."

Lars looked at the crate. The pale blue seam along the shard brightened faintly, not enough for the shuttle cameras, enough for him.

"Stop scanning it."

"Why?"

"Because I said so." Grim was three months old and already arguing about reasons.

The shuttle docked at the lab ring. Lars carried the crate himself, not because he needed to but because the station cameras expected rich idiots to be possessive about their purchases. The corridor outside his rented manufacturing bay smelled of hot printers, coolant, and Professor Fayolle's coffee.

Inside the lab, Fastulv's unfinished ribs climbed toward the ceiling. Grim's tanks stood behind a transparent shield near the core integration platform: six small cylinders of nutrient suspension, neural tissue lattices pulsing faintly, superconducting loops braided between them, status lights moving in calm patterns that Lars had designed to look less like a brain having opinions.

"Home," Grim said through the lab speakers.

"You have never left."

"Then you were home late."

Lars set the crate down. For one second, reflected in the pressure glass behind Grim's tanks, he saw a tall dark figure standing still in the lab doorway. He turned. Nothing. The door log showed no entry. The corridor camera showed an empty hall. The lab's thermal map showed a cooling trace where something had stood for less than two seconds.

One of the floor motes near the doorway flagged contamination, then corrected itself. Lars enlarged the maintenance feed. A dusting of gray-black particles lay across three centimeters of floor plating, too regular for dirt, too active for ash. The particles crawled inward, folded into themselves, and lost mass in the span of five heartbeats. Then they were gone.

"Tell me you recorded that," Lars said.

"I recorded that. And the recording now shows only sensor noise."

"Black Hole?"

"Insufficient data."

"A protector?"

"Insufficient data."

Lars looked at the black shard in the crate. Then the thought slid away. The lock did its work.

"Let's install the useless junk," he said.

Grim's tank lights brightened. "Finally."

The memory blurred at the edges. He was standing in Simulation Chamber 3, staring at nothing. Four months before Fastulv first left SPECULOOS station, holding parts he did not know he recognized.
