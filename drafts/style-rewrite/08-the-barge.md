## Chapter 8: The Barge

Lars woke to wind, which he had not asked for. He opened one eye and found himself under a hard yellow sun, lying on a narrow bunk inside a wooden cabin that smelled of tar, damp rope, old coffee, and engine oil. Something rattled above him. Water slapped against a hull. A gull screamed.

For three seconds he was sure Station Zero had drowned. Then the ceiling dissolved into a line of pale blue text: SIMULATION CHAMBER 3. REST INTERVAL COMPLETE. NON-BEEPING WAKE PROTOCOL SUCCESSFUL.

"Ödesvaka."

A small spherical drone hovered outside the open cabin door, backlit by sunlight. "Yes."

"Why am I on a boat?"

"Rest environment selected from recovered comfort index."

"This boat is shaking, and the sun is stabbing me, and I asked for coffee staged at the bed."

A small hatch opened beside the bunk. A metal arm extended with a steaming cup. Lars took it and sat up slowly. His body felt better, not good, better. The chemical weakness from the chamber had retreated into the background, and the strange internal hum of his repaired systems had steadied. His mind still felt overcrowded, but the old lock-trigger pain was gone. The forbidden words no longer struck him. They waited.

He stepped out of the cabin onto a barge in the middle of Mälaren, or a reconstruction of Mälaren, a very good one. The water glittered under the sun. Wind dragged small waves across the surface. The shoreline sat low and green in the distance. Rigging knocked against metal. The barge's engine vibrated through the deck. Grim stood at the bow. Valdrun came out of the wheelhouse carrying a glass filled with a gray liquid and black floating lumps.

Lars looked at it. "Breakfast?"

"Breakfast," Valdrun said, and glanced around the barge. Wind tore at his dark coat. In the sunlight his blue-pale skin looked even less human. "Is this a memory?"

"Apparently."

"A good one?"

Lars watched the water move. He remembered nothing specific, no childhood on Earth, no real barge trip, no single clean event, but the scene carried a composite comfort: yellow sunlight, engine vibration, cold water, coffee strong enough to make moral arguments. Maybe his cover life had borrowed from actual visits to Earth. Maybe his mind had chosen old Sweden as a quiet place to store things too large for sterile rooms.

"Good enough," he said.

They sat at a bolted metal table on deck. A planning model appeared above it, projected by the simulation, the Milky Way as a slow-turning cloud of lights, colored not by politics but by risk: red for active predation, amber for systemic fragility, blue for hidden nodes, green for self-sufficient clusters. There was too little green.

"I completed the initial archive index," Grim said. "While you slept."

"Findings?"

"Lyra's reactivation criteria have crossed the first threshold. Possibly the second, depending on how we read Syren Collective fragmentation. I would count threshold two. Likely hypnotized Aerits. Vigor sent a delayed packet after we left The Rim. He knows you entered the Void. He does not know whether you reached Station Zero. The packet contains jokes, two warnings, one invoice for elderflower cordial Grim added without authorization, and a guardian-authenticated offer of assistance."

"I told him he could have the cordial," Lars said.

Valdrun smiled into his drink. "Vigor always did prefer cover identities with kitchens."

"That farm was a working example of Återviens."

He said the word for the first time. It felt old in his mouth.

The planning model changed. Green points brightened where decentralized systems still functioned: independent arkologies, hidden farms, mobile habitats, autonomous AI clusters, small colonies with closed-loop energy and food systems, cultures that had remembered restraint without remembering who taught it.

"Lyra's term," Valdrun said. "Or one of them. It began as a resilience index. Then a design framework. Then it spread on purpose, in stories, engineering standards, rituals, old songs, school lessons, food systems, architecture, and boring maintenance manuals."

"The boring manuals survived best," Grim said.

"Most of civilization is maintenance," Lars said.

Valdrun pointed at the model. "Återviens measures whether a system can take a hit, shed extra complexity, get back on its feet, and keep going without becoming a centralized monster."

"Jevons," Lars said.

The model shifted to a historical overlay: coal, oil, fusion, antimatter, computation, simulation economies, habitat megastructures, energy markets. Each time something got cheaper to run, people built more of it, and the whole stack grew more dependent.

Grim talked through it the way Lars had once talked through a plasma problem at a board. When energy became cheap, the galaxy did not become free. It built larger cages. Megastations that needed synchronized supply chains. Gas giant cities that needed constant thermal management. Simulated luxury worlds using star-scale energy budgets to keep bored aristocrats entertained. Private defense grids so complex they needed wars to justify their own maintenance.

"Aphex is on that last part of the chart," Grim added.

"Of course it is."

Valdrun leaned forward. "Lyra's point was not that building is wrong. She built more than any of us. Every added layer has to justify its complexity cost. Every convenience against dependency. Every center has to have a way to break apart without killing the whole."

Lars watched the red zones pulse. "And the galaxy ignored her."

"Some did. Some could not hear the lesson without hearing the name behind it. Some remembered the lesson as culture and forgot the source. That was partly intended."

"Because if they remembered Aerits, they would hunt us."

"Yes."

"And now they are hunting us anyway."

"Some are."

Grim expanded the Black Hole B-7 data. "They cannot use what they are looking for, not as an organization. Penal hierarchy, secrecy, fear, duplicated surveillance, loyalty tests. Any useful understanding collapses before it can spread."

Lars sipped coffee and looked at the red nodes. "So if they got my blood, they would not get a golden empire."

"No," Valdrun said. "They would get dead researchers, corrupted hosts, containment failures, and enough partial results to keep trying."

The barge pitched in the simulated wind. Lars caught an empty cup before it slid off the table.

"What is the plan?"

Grim moved the model. "Immediate: repair Station Zero's auxiliary chamber, re-enable safe communication paths, inform Vigor without exposing the node, and move Fastulv, the new freight ship, inside the bubble before The Rim traffic logs become interesting."

"Good."

"Near term: complete your staged unlock, verify your memory integrity, establish guardian contact with the remaining five known channels, and identify hypnotized carriers under active threat."

"You said five known channels."

Valdrun answered. "There are seven guardians. Lyra's channel is not a normal channel anymore. Your parents' channels are dormant but not confirmed dead. Vigor is reachable. I am here. The remaining two are buried behind conditions I cannot safely open until your next stage."

"That is irritating."

"Yes."

"I assume it is also wise."

"Usually."

Lars looked at the long-term model. Wake hidden nodes, reinforce vulnerable carriers, reduce dependency in selected colonies, seed better cultural patterns, expose hostile acquisition networks, avoid a dramatic center, and do not announce the return of a half-mythical enhanced human lineage with ancient nanotechnology.

"We don't need an empire," he said. "We need the hidden nodes awake, the carriers protected, the colonies able to stand without a center."

Valdrun's eyes sharpened. Grim looked up from the model.

"What?"

"That phrasing appears in three Lyra archive branches," Grim said.

"Of course it does."

Lars watched the simulated water.

"Next archive," he said.

"After food," Grim said. "Coffee is not food."

Valdrun stood. "The simulation can produce lunch."
