## Chapter 7: Pluto Protocol 9

The archive lay below the medical ring and above the memory vault. They reached it through a lift that did not so much travel as change where they were; one moment the door sealed in the medical corridor, the next it opened into a chamber like a shallow amphitheater, black glass and white stone and space-gray ribs, with pale blue filaments hanging in the air like frozen rain. There were no shelves and no drives, only the chamber, the filaments, and a central table grown out of the floor.

"Where is the archive?" Grim asked.

Valdrun pointed around them. "Here. The light is the files."

The filaments brightened when Lars stepped forward. Each one resolved into dense encoded structure: what the file was, who had opened it, how damaged it was, medical warnings, and a map of what sat inside. Most of it sat in light an ordinary human would not have seen; the visible blue was only leakage. Grim recalibrated his optics, for the fifth time that day, and muttered that he was getting tired of eyes.

Valdrun stood at the central table. "We start with Pluto Protocol 9."

Lars stopped. The word Pluto pulled at him, a cold world at the edge of the old Solar System, a buried object, a woman touching something she did not yet understand.

"How old is the record?"

Valdrun brought up the file stamp. "Original capture: 2032, Pluto artifact zone, ISEA isolated module four. Copied and annotated several times. This copy was sealed into Station Zero's staged reactivation archive after Alpha Fourteen began."

Lars did the math the way he always did, without deciding to. "Seven hundred and forty-two years."

"Approximately, depending on which clock standard you use."

The chamber dimmed and a holojournal formed above the table, at first a weak blue ghost, then, as his eyes adjusted, temperature noise, radiation scatter, camera correction, later overlays of microscopic emission, microtremor analysis, neural stress inferred from posture and voice.

The image showed a small room under harsh light. Ice dust clung to the seams of the equipment cases. A pressure door sat behind the subject, white at the edges, and beyond a narrow observation slit lay a darkness that felt cold even in reconstruction. Lyra Vargstjärna stood beside a workbench in a thermal suit, cropped dark hair, sharp cheekbones, eyes that moved too quickly. A tired scientist.

For a moment nobody in the archive spoke. Then the old recording began.

"Pluto artifact zone. Isolation module four. ISEA internal log. Lyra Vargstjärna recording. Timestamp: mission day... irrelevant. Local clocks have drifted twice since the last incident. Use the corrected packet header."

Grim leaned a little toward Lars and whispered that he liked her. Lars told him to be quiet.

Lyra rubbed her face with both hands. "This began with contact. I touched the object because I thought the outer layer was inert. That was wrong. It responded."

A second layer opened beside her, microscopic footage added later, particles moving through blood plasma.

"We found the first machines in my bloodstream thirty-seven hours later. I am using the word machines because nanobots is both imprecise and emotionally unhelpful. They did not behave like infection. They did not replicate blindly. They mapped. Waited. Adjusted."

She paused, and her eyes flicked to something off-screen.

"I am stronger. Reflex tests are outside human range. Visual response has expanded into ultraviolet and adjacent bands. Radiation damage from yesterday's suit breach has repaired faster than current medicine can explain. I held breath for twenty-six minutes before terminating the trial because Dr. Harrow threatened to sedate me."

Lars felt his own skin prickle. Grim looked at him and said nothing.

"The voices are not voices. I need that in the record. No auditory hallucination. No linguistic content at first. More like weighted probabilities arriving as intuition. Images. Structural relationships. Sometimes a solution appears before I know the question."

Lars stared at the recording. His mouth had gone dry. He knew that feeling from the inside, from napkins and coffee and problems that solved themselves before he had finished forming the question.

The recording shifted to a data overlay, superconducting loops, quantum noise irregularities, correlations beyond known communication limits.

"The artifact is not just an object. It is a node. We thought the unknown civilization had left a machine on Pluto as a test or beacon. That may still be partly true. But the machines inside me are not merely transferring stored knowledge. They are attempting handshake with something larger."

Lyra smiled then, without much humor. "I hate that sentence. It sounds like religion. We have signal correlations, nonlocal timing anomalies, and enough reproducible data to make three physicists angry."

Lars let his breath out. Valdrun glanced at him.

Lyra looked down at her hands. "The immediate problem is practical. Earth-side actors already know we found something. Corporate probes, state military assets, religious lunatics pretending to be environmentalists, and at least one private fleet are maneuvering toward Pluto. They do not understand what this is. That makes them more dangerous, not less."

The room behind her flickered. An alarm flashed red, then stopped.

"If this line continues, the artifact will become a prize. If it becomes a prize, the worst people in the system will define the next century."

Later annotations slid in at the edge of the image: Earth Reclaimers, early technooligarchic coalitions, classified military intercepts, failed sabotage attempts. Lyra's voice became quieter.

"So we need survival here. Controlled replication of the machines only to vetted team members. And the search for the spacetime distortion beyond Pluto's orbital plane. The artifact points toward something, but not like a map. More like a key changes the shape of the lock around it."

Grim whispered, "Portal." Valdrun did not correct him.

Lyra leaned closer to the recorder. "If anyone is watching this centuries from now, do not assume we understood it. We did not. We were frightened, exhausted, and moving too fast because the alternative was letting idiots with flags and quarterly targets touch a million-year-old system with dirty hands."

Her expression softened for the first time. "But I think the machines are not hostile. That is not trust. That is a working hypothesis. They cooperate with the host. They preserve function. They improve survival. They appear to prefer minds capable of doubt."

She looked away, listening to someone outside the frame. "Yes, Pierre, I am putting that in the log. No, your objections are not more important because you say them in French."

The recording continued.

"Final note for this entry. We are making decisions too quickly. That may be unavoidable, but I want the record to show the danger. The base cannot depend on one reactor, one food loop, one air plant, one authority chain, or one person who happens to be infected with alien machines and too tired to sleep."

The annotation layer marked the section with later tags: Pluto survival doctrine, pre-Heliostat resilience instinct, Lyra private framework.

Her tired face hardened. "For now this is not philosophy. It is plumbing. If one valve fails, we breathe. If one greenhouse dies, we eat. If I die, the others continue. If Earth cuts supply, Pluto does not. We can argue about grand principles when nothing is actively trying to kill us."

The recording ended. The holojournal collapsed back into pale blue filaments, and the archive chamber returned. For a while nobody spoke. Lars stared at the empty space above the table where she had been.

Grim broke the silence first. "She reminds me of you."

"Be quiet."

Valdrun rested both hands on the table. "That log is before Heliostat. Before the Kalari. Before the Rakh'Na attack in 2083. Before the word Återviens. The strain that made the doctrine possible."

Lars looked at the filaments. "Show me the later branch."

Valdrun studied him. "You just learned your age. You just watched the beginning of everything that made you possible. You need rest."

"I need a plan."

Grim turned from the display. "I can run archive indexing while you sleep."

Valdrun opened another layer. "The later branch contains Lyra's reactivation criteria. Threshold models. Guardian contact protocols. Known hidden nodes. Risk estimates for hostile acquisition of nanobot carriers."

"Black Hole B-7."

"Among others."

"Earth Reclaimers?"

"Still present, in mutated forms."

"Syren Collective?"

Valdrun's face became grim. "Worse than present."

Lars rubbed his eyes.

"Fine. Grim indexes. Valdrun sleeps, or repairs, after we pulled him out from under that distributor. I rest for two hours."

"Six," Grim said.

"Four, and coffee staged at the bed."

Grim considered. "Acceptable."

Ödesvaka, which had followed them silently into the archive, rotated in the air. "Rest interval logged."

Lars looked up at it. "If you wake me with beeping, we will have a problem."

"Wake protocol adjusted to non-beeping."

Valdrun laughed. It was the first full laugh Lars had heard from him.
