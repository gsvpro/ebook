# Chapter 23: The Frenchman's Ledger

Gabriel Arsenault received Lars in a room high inside Aphex Defence's Paris orbital branch. The windows showed Earth. The carpet muffled footsteps. The air smelled of paper and polish. Old campaign maps on one wall. Legal screens on the other.

The desk was real wood.

Lars set a greasy paper bag on it. Grim set a second beside the first.

Arsenault stared. "Monsieur Odin, is that food on a seventeenth-century desk?"

"Breakfast."

"It is fourteen hundred hours and the desk has survived wars."

"We were hungry."

Grim took out a pastry and turned it in his fingers, looking at the layers. Arsenault watched crumbs near the wood and said nothing for a moment.

The Aphex director had sent three messages since L-Prime. One demanded a report. One offered support. One contained an old NUKEA procurement trail connected to the dead infiltrator's coordinate burst, the one that had pointed toward the quiet region. The official report lay unopened on the desk between pastry grease and the wood.

"You called it a containment drill," Arsenault said.

"Fabron did."

"Major Fabron also filed an addendum describing remote command delay as a hostile collaborator."

"Good addendum."

"It has caused an internal review."

"Even better."

Arsenault's smile showed no pleasure. "You have placed my corporation in the uncomfortable position of being correct for the wrong reasons."

"Try not to make a habit of it."

The room went quiet enough that the ventilation was audible. Lars saw the tiredness under Arsenault's eyes then. A man measuring the distance between what he wanted and a cliff.

Arsenault opened a wall vault and removed a paper ledger.

It was bound in cracked leather, reinforced along the spine, ugly with repairs. Real ink. Real tabs. Fingerprints at the corners.

"Before you begin vandalizing my dignity, note that this survived eight network collapses, three hostile audits, and one divorce."

"My money is on the divorce."

"A correct assessment."

The ledger held shell suppliers, minority investments, rescue grants, military redundancies, and reactor-hardened agricultural habitats sold at a loss under charitable fronts. It also held names: long-lived engineers, survivors who should not have survived, smugglers moving medicine without invoices, soldiers who had disobeyed remote orders during system failures and were later protected by someone who should not have known.

Each name had context. A coolant crisis on Abantu Station Twelve. A famine convoy that should have been intercepted and was not. A child immunization program funded through a mining insurance rebate. A maintenance guild that refused a profitable standardization scheme and then survived a cascade that killed three competitors.

Lars turned the pages carefully.

"You were tracking hidden people," Lars said.

"I was tracking anomalies that correlated with institutional survival," Arsenault said. "Do not assume my ignorance lacked rigor."

"You didn't know what you had."

"I knew I had a pattern that made fragile systems less fragile. In my profession that is a religion if one is poor and a portfolio if one is honest."

The word Återviens appeared in seventeen spellings. Sometimes it was written as discipline, sometimes as superstition, guild law, rural etiquette, a reactor prayer, a mess-hall joke. Sometimes it was only a mark beside a supply route, or a family saying, or the habit of refusing to let a single authority hold the only key.

Fabron's Clear Hand appeared twice, once as a charitable maintenance order and once as a minor security nuisance.

"Careful," Lars said. "You wrote down an armed doctrine."

"Major Fabron would object to the word church."

"He objects professionally."

Arsenault poured coffee from a silver pot and did not offer any to Grim.

"I treated Återviens as a useful infection," Arsenault said. "Systems touched by it failed less stupidly."

"You tried to buy it."

"Of course. It resisted purchase, which improved both my admiration and my temper."

"Did you try to own the people?"

Arsenault set the cup down. "I have tried to own many things. These, not successfully. A scholarship here. A contract there. Protection with invoices attached. Some of my subsidiaries behaved as predators. Some behaved as shields. I am not offering sainthood. I am offering a ledger before someone worse uses a cleaner one."

Lars wanted to hate that answer more. It was useful.

Grim slid a page closer. "The quiet accounts keep turning up near the places where someone tried to buy the pattern. No owner, or the owner is masked, or the owner is listed as a non-person. Someone besides Aphex made a ledger without this furniture."

The final section was worse. NUKEA compliance shells, Syren charities, Black Hole brokers, and ownerless quiet accounts had circled the same patterns. They had noticed where local hands kept systems alive.

Arsenault slid a thin recall dossier across the desk.

A NUKEA recall had been moved forward across twelve marginal colonies. One of them, Havskant, sat near a misfiled portal anomaly and a cluster of ledger names. The recall was packaged as safety. Its funding came through three shells and a public pressure campaign about reactor harmonization. The timing matched the med-bay coordinate burst by seventeen hours.

"The recall is real," Arsenault said. "The timing is engineered. People with budgets use that difference."

Grim skimmed the recall dossier and stopped chewing pastry.

"The new firmware can put every reactor's emergency switch on one key," he said. "If that key is turned, all twelve governors take orders from the same place."

"NUKEA promised that was impossible."

"They lied, or they hoped."

Arsenault leaned forward. "Aphex central contains factions that would put Dr. Sovan in a sealed lab and invoice it as protection. NUKEA contains factions that would license survival by the quarter. Black Hole would harvest it. Syren would write hymns over the corpse. I am greedy, Monsieur Odin, not suicidal."

"I am moved. Mostly toward nausea."

"Practical," Arsenault said. "Help me make Aphex useful before Aphex becomes the problem."

Lars looked out at Earth. The planet below had survived empires, plagues, Rakh'Na, and people renaming old mistakes as strategy. Somewhere under the clouds, old Aerit decisions had become village habits, soldier discipline, reactor checklists, and soup before speeches.

"Mira stays out of your custody," Lars said.

"Agreed."

"Fabron keeps local authority over L-Prime security."

"He will become intolerable."

"He already is."

"You ask much for a man eating on my desk."

"I'm about to ask more. You will leak the right amount of fear into NUKEA channels. Not Aerit. Not KBC wonder. Boring engineering liability."

Arsenault's eyes sharpened. "Boring fear travels farther in committees."

"Exactly. Make them afraid of being sued by physics."

Lars took the recall dossier. "Fine. But if I catch you harvesting anomalies, I turn this desk into a canoe."

Arsenault looked genuinely pained. "That threat is more effective than you deserve."

At the door Lars paused. The oldest page lay open behind him. In the margin beside an old entry about Lyra Vargstjärna's Pluto find, someone had copied a three-line translation from a much older source. The handwriting was not his. A clerk, or an ancestor, or an early Aphex watcher had tried to translate a fragment of language near Återviens first into French, then into trade English. The corrections were messy. The writer had been afraid of making it sound like a religion, and afraid of making it sound too small.

Old engineers passing through.

Lars left.
