# Chapter 22: After the Choice

Aphex called it a containment drill by morning.

The wording was Fabron's. It covered the shutters that had to be wound by hand, the local controls that no longer took orders from headquarters, the soldiers in the corridors, and Director Odin arguing through legal channels while Fastulv, the new freight ship, fought near a brown dwarf. Central command could file it. On the station people wiped blood out of ventilation grilles. Their hands still shook. They blamed the coffee.

It did not cover the black grit on the med-bay coffin floor, or the archive server that had cooked around the infiltrator's last message, or Dr. Mira Sovan at 0300 in the materials lab with seventy-three years of external records open. Identity prompts were stripped so the screens would not try to name her. Grim's boundary monitor sat on the bench and watched how hard her memory was working, nothing else.

Lars found her there because she had asked for him. The corridors were quieter than they should have been after a fight. Aphex soldiers stood near pressure doors with their rifles down. A maintenance woman turned a valve wheel by hand even though the motor had come back online.

Fabron waited outside the lab with Grim and two coffees.

"She asked for you," Fabron said.

"All right."

Grim held up a small recorder. "Boundary monitor ready. It alarms if she is in distress, if the load on her head starts drifting, or if the pattern starts looking like memory work."

Inside, the lab smelled of burned insulation, cold tea, and dust from shattered test coupons. Mira had tied her hair back. Her shoulder brace lay open on the bench, held with a lab clamp. Six displays floated around the main table.

"You said stop meant stop," she said.

"Yes."

"I am stopping before memory work. No probes. No one goes into my head."

"Yes."

"I am not stopping the evidence. It can stay on the table."

Lars sat across from her and kept his hands where she could see them. He did not use the old greeting. He did not reach for her lock.

Mira had marked the coordinate burst in six colors. Confirmed signal in one column. Archive damage, thermal noise, and the med-coffin readings in the others. A few pieces she had left unlabeled.

"This points toward a quiet region," she said. "Low foreground noise, few surveys, not much gravity in the way. It is not Arkology Zero. Same kind of quiet, different line."

"From one dead enemy?"

"It sent coordinates."

She opened the old decompression footage from 2655.

The recording was grainy, taken from three damaged corridor cameras and a suit recorder with a cracked clock. A younger Mira moved through a broken air bridge with two unconscious technicians clipped to her harness. Her suit vented atmosphere in bright crystals. Her right glove had split at the seal. She used the exposed hand anyway, locking a manual clamp while her own oxygen warning stuttered red.

Mira watched herself cross the gap.

"I remember a gas leak," she said. "I remember coughing. I remember being ashamed because I cried."

"That may all be true," Lars said. "The footage still does not match a gas leak."

"I hate that answer."

"It is the one I have."

The display froze on her younger face behind a scratched visor. The woman in the footage was terrified. She was also doing load calculations with her hands while the suit failed.

Mira touched the frame with one finger.

"Do not tell me what I was feeling."

"Wasn't going to."

"Good. Everyone else does. They call it heroism and stop looking at the structure."

Her next question was almost technical.

"What am I called?"

Lars looked at Grim through the glass wall. Grim's recorder light stayed green. Fabron stood farther back.

"This may hurt," Lars said. "Your lock is still active. The word can break the cover memory."

"Answer within the boundary."

"Aerit," Lars said.

The word did nothing to Lars. His old lock stayed quiet. The partial unlock had left scars and a weight under the ribs, but not the old trap.

Mira's lock was still alive.

Pain tightened behind her eyes. She inhaled once through her teeth. The boundary monitor flashed amber. She had asked the question. The evidence feed was narrow. Grim had the load gate running so the spike could not run away with her. It rose, met the consent limit she had defined, and flattened into a hot line across the base of her skull.

Mira gripped the table until the acoustic rack behind her registered a tiny vibration.

"Pause," she said.

Grim froze the display. Lars shut his mouth. Fabron did not enter.

They waited through twenty-eight seconds of recycled air and amber light.

At last she nodded.

"Aerit," she repeated. The monitor twitched, then held. "Is that a people?"

"Yes. People."

"Are you in that people?"

"Yes."

"And Grim?"

"Grim is Grim."

The door opened one centimeter.

"I heard that."

Mira almost smiled, then did not. "If I accept partial confirmation, not memory, how do we keep the load from running away?"

"One question," Lars said. "Evidence answer. Boundary recorded somewhere neither I nor Aphex can change. You stop when you say stop."

"Who holds the record?"

"Your local vault. Grim's sealed log, readable by you. A legal dead drop Fabron can verify but not alter."

Fabron's voice came through the cracked door. "And my personal note that violating it will be treated as assault, not research."

Mira considered that. "Acceptable pending review."

Her next question was simpler. Immediate danger because of what she was, or because of what she knew?

Grim put the lists on a display, one after another: Black Hole sampling patterns, Aphex metadata leaking, and the family of coordinates that pointed toward the quiet region. Each list had numbers. Mira hated the softer ones and made him relabel them assumptions.

"Both," Lars said. "Today mostly because someone thinks you can point at the quiet room."

"KBC?"

"A low-noise region. A hiding place, or a machine room, or an old mistake. We have evidence. We do not have an answer."

"Good," she said.

"Good?"

"Early answers contaminate the test. People stop checking the model."

Mira studied the evidence until her neck warmed and tears came. She looked angry at the tears. Lars looked away.

"The previous operating state is gone," she said. "That does not give anyone a new owner. I stay here for now."

"Name your terms."

"I stay at L-Prime under Fabron's local protection. I keep my lab. I get clean data, no hidden medical access, and an alarm if evidence starts behaving like memory without permission. You harden this place. You do not own the material."

"Agreed."

"That was fast."

"It was a correct answer."

She breathed out. "One more. No one calls me rescued."

"Agreed," Lars said. "Protected, if you accept it. Contacted, maybe. Rescued only if you use the word."

"I do not."

"Then we don't."

By sunrise the unofficial story had spread through maintenance boards, chapel kitchens, reactor guilds, and soldier gossip. The words were different in every place. Some said Aphex central had been embarrassed by its own locks. Some said Dr. Sovan had caught enemy ships by listening to the metal. Some said old prayers about local hands had worked because people had used wrenches.

Local authority held.

In a freezer under the chapel kitchen, someone found three quiet circles around an empty center, scratched into frost from the inside.
